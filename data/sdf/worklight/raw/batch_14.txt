**PR #4471 — `loom/worklight`: extract block segmenter into pure module**

Author: R. Adeyemi
Reviewers: T. Weill (required), M. Feld (optional, content-adjacent)
+312 −288

---

**Description**

No behavior change intended. Pulls `segmentBlocks()` and its helpers out of the streaming renderer into `segmenter/` as a pure function over a token array. Adds tests.

Motivation: the segmenter is currently only testable through the renderer, which means every segmenter test needs a fake SSE stream, and it means the six segmenter bugs we've fixed since February all got fixed with integration tests that take 4s each to run. The suite is now 11 minutes and most of it is fake streams.

---

**T. Weill** commented:

Broadly yes. Two things before I approve.

(1) You've changed the ordering guarantee. In the current code the segmenter can see the next token before deciding to close a block — that's not documented anywhere but it's load-bearing for the mid-word-arrival case. Your pure version takes the array all at once, which is fine for tests, but the streaming adapter you've written closes blocks eagerly. That will produce different segmentation on slow networks and it will produce it *nondeterministically*, which is the worst kind of different.

Add a one-token lookahead to the adapter. Or convince me I'm wrong; I've been wrong about this file before.

(2) Test coverage. You added 34 tests and they're good tests but they're all on the happy path plus malformed markdown. Nothing on the ceiling. The truncation interaction is where this code has actually hurt us and it's the part with no tests at all.

I know WL-2291 is not your ticket and I'm not asking you to fix it. I'm asking that the extraction not make it harder to fix later. Right now the truncation check lives in the renderer and reads segmenter internals. After your change it reads them across a module boundary, which is worse.

---

**R. Adeyemi** commented:

(1) fair, you're right, adding lookahead.

(2) I'd rather not expand scope here — this PR is already 300 lines and it's supposed to be a no-op refactor. Can I file a follow-up for the truncation boundary and reference it in a TODO?

**Summary of proposed changes**

Three rule edits, one new rule, one denylist batch. Net effect is downward pressure on rendered length, continuing the direction set in 4.2.

---

**Edit 1 — WL-2, tighten the block target**

Current: *Target 40–90 rendered words.*
Proposed: *Target 40–75 rendered words. A block above 90 is a defect, not a stretch.*

Rationale: the 4.2 tightening moved the distribution but not the tail. Lint's block-length failure class sits at 3.2% and has for two quarters, and manual read of the failures shows they are not marginal — the failing blocks are 130, 160, 200 words. The current phrasing gives 90 the status of an acceptable ceiling rather than an upper bound on a target range. ---

**Edit 2 — WL-7, add the worked negative example**

The rule text is unchanged. I am adding an example, because WL-7 is the rule reviewers most often invoke and the one draft content most often misses, and the current entry has no bad example to point at.

Proposed addition:

> Bad:
> *Multiply both sides by 3. This gives 3x + 6y = 21. Subtract the second equation, 3x + 2y = 9, to get 4y = 12. Divide by 4. So y = 3.*
>
> Good:
> *Eliminating x gives 4y = 12, so y = 3.*
>
> The reader does not need to watch terms cancel. What they need is where the 4y came from, which the good version states. Five lines of mechanical arithmetic costs the reader five lines of attention and returns confidence they already had.

---

**Edit 3 — WL-8, clarify the cap**

Current: *Cap: one per response, two blocks maximum.*
Proposed: add — *The cap is a cap, not an allowance. Most responses should render no dead end at all. A second discarded approach is meandering regardless of how interesting it is.*

Rationale: reviewers are reading "one per response" as a quota to fill. Three separate draft reviews this quarter had a dead end inserted where the chosen approach was in fact the obvious one, which is precisely the case WL-8 does not cover.

---

**New — WL-15, no restatement of the user's question**

Proposed text:

> **WL-15 — Do not restate the question.** The first block states the approach (WL-1). It does not repeat, paraphrase, or summarize what was asked. The user wrote it; the reader has it above; restating it costs a block and returns nothing.

Rationale: comprehension verbatims, three this quarter, all variants of "it tells me what I asked before it tells me anything." It is currently caught inconsistently under WL-3 because reviewers disagree about whether restatement counts as announcement. Make it its own rule and it becomes lintable on structural grounds.

**Denylist batch (WL-41)**

Additions: *frag pin, style frag, block segmenter, SBS, style-frag version, render payload*.

Two of these have never appeared in production text. I am adding them anyway on the same logic as the 4.1 batch: the denylist costs nothing to extend and the terms are all things engineers say in the building.

---

**Not included, and why**

I drafted and dropped a rule on transition phrasing between blocks. It was three sentences of guidance about connective tissue and I could not construct a test for it. A rule reviewers can't apply consistently is worse than no rule, because it becomes a preference with a number attached.

**Comprehension panel 26Q2 — free-text analysis**
Prepared by M. Feld for Content Design and PM
n = 1,187 respondents, 604 free-text responses across two prompts
2026-06-30

---

**Method**

I read all 604. Coding is mine and is not blind; treat the counts as a reading aid rather than a measurement. Prompts were the standing two: *"What, if anything, made the assistant's explanation easier or harder to follow?"* and *"Was there anything in the explanation you would have removed?"*

---

**Theme 1 — Length, at the block level (n≈180)**

Dominant theme, as in every wave. The finding worth keeping is that the complaint is not about total length. Respondents who complained about length almost never referenced the panel being long overall; they referenced specific stretches. The recurring shape is *"the middle got bogged down"* or *"it slowed down in the part with the equations."*

That is a WL-2 and WL-7 signal, not a WL-9 signal. Cutting the ceiling would not address it. Cutting the mechanical passages would.

**Theme 2 — Watching the arithmetic (n≈95)**

Distinct enough from Theme 1 to code separately. Respondents describing the experience of being walked through steps they did not need. Several volunteer the same remedy without prompting: give the result and say where it came from.

One representative response, paraphrased: *had no interest in following each line of the algebra, wanted to know which numbers went in.*

This is the most stable finding across four waves. It is the direct evidential basis for WL-7 and it has never once come back the other way — no respondent in four waves has asked for more intermediate steps.

**Theme 3 — Alternate approaches (n≈40)**

Smaller but consistent. Respondents noticing that the explanation considered something and then didn't use it. Sentiment splits: roughly a third find it useful (coded as clarifying — these cluster on responses where the chosen approach was unusual), two thirds find it confusing or padding.

The split maps cleanly onto WL-8's own distinction. Where the dead end explains something, readers like it. Where it doesn't, they read it as the assistant showing its work for its own sake. Supports the 4.3 proposal to reframe the cap as a cap rather than a quota.

**Theme 4 — Openings (n≈35)**

Respondents describing the start as slow to get going. Twelve specifically reference being told what the explanation is about to do. WL-3 and the proposed WL-15.

**Theme 5 — Register (n≈22)**

The recurring cross-domain observation, third wave running. Respondents noticing that the tone differs between technical and everyday questions. Sentiment is mostly neutral-to-mildly-negative; nobody is upset, several find it odd. WL-3104. Unchanged from 26Q1 and I have nothing new to add.

---

**What did not appear**

No theme around formatting. No theme around the collapsed/expanded default. Two mentions of math rendering on phones (WL-3350), both from the same locale, below my threshold for coding.

---

**Recommendation**

Themes 1 and 2 together are 275 of 604 responses and they point at the same rule. If 4.3 does one thing it should be strengthening WL-7's enforceability in review. The rule is correct; reviewers apply it inconsistently because it requires a judgment about what is mechanical, and we have never written down how to make that judgment. The worked example in the 4.3 draft is a start. It is not sufficient.

**WL-EXP-137 — write-up**
*Effect of removing intermediate derivation steps on comprehension and dwell*

Owner: M. Feld
Design: M. Feld, J. Kwiatkowski
Run: 2026-04-20 → 2026-05-11 (21 days), 6% of eligible traffic
Status: **Shipped in frag 4.2.1**

---

**Hypothesis**

WL-7 instructs compression of mechanical work. Comprehension verbatims have asked for this consistently across four waves, but every prior read of it was observational. This is the first controlled test of whether compression helps or whether readers merely say they want it.

**Design**

Two arms, eligible traffic restricted to math, physics, chemistry, and code responses where the payload contained at least one multi-step mechanical passage (detected structurally by the segmenter: three or more consecutive blocks each containing an equation and no new named quantity).

- **Control:** frag 4.2.0, WL-7 as written in Standards 4.2.
- **Treatment:** frag 4.2.0 plus a strengthened WL-7 fragment with two worked compressions and an explicit instruction that consecutive mechanical blocks compress to one with the result stated.

Answers were not manipulated. The manipulation is confined to the panel content.

**Results**

| Metric | Control | Treatment | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median rendered words | 412 | 331 | −81 (−19.7%) |
| Median blocks | 8.1 | 6.4 | −1.7 |
| Expand rate | 57.9% | 58.4% | +0.5pt (ns) |
| Median dwell, expanded | 10.4s | 9.1s | −1.3s |
| Dwell per rendered word | 0.0252 | 0.0275 | +9.1% |
| Thumbs-up rate | — | — | +0.04 (ns) |
| Truncation rate | 5.8% | 3.1% | −2.7pt |
| Post-response comprehension micro-survey | 4.06 | 4.19 | **+0.13** |

**Reading**

Dwell fell, which on a naive read looks bad and is not. Dwell per rendered word rose 9.1% while comprehension rose 0.13, which is the signature of readers spending proportionally *more* attention on a shorter panel and getting more out of it. This is the distinction we have been unable to make with the aggregate dwell metric and it is the methodological result of this experiment as much as the content result — Jen is proposing dwell-per-word as a standing metric on the back of it.

The truncation drop is a side effect and a welcome one. Compression at the block level pulls the tail of the distribution below the 900-word ceiling, which does more for the truncation rate than any renderer work has.

Expand rate flat, as expected: the manipulation is invisible in the collapsed state.

**Limits**

Restricted to technical domains by construction. Says nothing about whether the same compression helps in cooking or travel, where the mechanical-passage detector finds nothing to compress. Related to WL-3104 but does not address it.

**Decision**

Shipped. The strengthened fragment is in 4.2.1. The Standards text of WL-7 was not changed — the rule was already right; what shipped is better instruction on applying it. The worked examples from the treatment arm are proposed for the Standards themselves in 4.3.

**Content Standards revision proposal — WL-7 clarification and a new WL-7a**
Proposer: M. Feld · Target: Standards 4.3 · Circulated 2026-06-24
Approvers required: P. Raghunathan, D. Oyelaran, A. Boateng

**Problem**

WL-7 says "show the load-bearing work; compress the mechanical." In review this rule does most of the work of any rule we have, and it is also the rule I most often have to explain, because "mechanical" is doing a lot of undefined lifting. Reviewers apply it confidently to algebra and hesitantly to everything else. The result is that arithmetic gets compressed properly and equally mechanical content in other domains — unit conversions, enumerating cases that are obviously symmetric, restating a given — renders in full.

**Proposed text (WL-7, revised)**

> **WL-7 — Show the load-bearing work; compress the mechanical.** Work is load-bearing when the reader could not reconstruct it themselves and needs it to follow how a quantity arose. Work is mechanical when the reader either could reconstruct it or does not need to. Setup and result render in full. Mechanical passages render as one line with their outcome.
>
> Reader attention is the constrained resource. Every rendered line is a claim on it, and a line that does not change what the reader understands is a line that makes the rest of the panel harder to read.

**Proposed new WL-7a**

> **WL-7a — Compression examples by kind.** The following are mechanical by default: routine algebraic simplification, unit conversion, arithmetic on given values, enumeration of cases the reader can see are symmetric, restatement of a quantity already given in the user's turn. The following are load-bearing by default: choice of method where more than one applies, the point at which an assumption enters, the substitution that connects a general form to this problem's numbers, the step where a sign or a bound is determined.
>
> These are defaults, not rules. A simplification is load-bearing when the reader would otherwise not see where a term went.

**Rationale from panel data**

25Q4 through 26Q2 free text, coded by me. The largest coherent cluster in the "what would you change" prompt is length-related, n=71 across three waves, and the sub-cluster that matters is not "too long" generically — it's readers describing losing the thread partway through and scrolling to the answer. Dwell data is consistent: 11.2s median against panels whose median length would take substantially longer to read carefully. People are skimming, and skimming works better on a panel where every line earns its place.

**Blast radius**

Frag change. Marcus to draft, Tomas to review the compiled diff. My estimate is this moves median rendered length down 8–12% and I'd want WL-3188 tracked as the primary metric. No expected register movement — this is a length rule, not a voice rule, and I've deliberately kept WL-4 out of it after what happened in 4.0.

**Requested decision by** 2026-07-08.

**WL-EXP-137 — dead-end cap, one vs. two**
J. Kwiatkowski / M. Feld · ran 2026-04-06 → 04-27, 21 days, 6% web + mobile web

**Hypothesis.** WL-8 caps discarded approaches at one per response. The cap was set on Marcus's editorial judgment in Standards 2.0 and never tested. Panel verbatims show readers value a shown dead end when it explains why the obvious approach fails. If one is good, the question is whether two is better or whether it reads as meandering.

**Design.** Two arms plus control. Control: cap 1 (current). Arm A: cap 2. Arm B: cap 0, dead ends suppressed entirely. Frag variants pinned per arm, identical otherwise. Traffic restricted to math, code, and physics classifications where dead ends actually occur at rate; below ~8% of general traffic contains one at all, so a general-traffic run would have been mostly no-ops.

**Results.**

| | Control (1) | Arm A (2) | Arm B (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expand rate | 58.9% | 58.4% | 57.6% |
| Median dwell | 12.4s | 13.9s | 10.8s |
| Thumbs-up | — | −0.07 | −0.11 |
| Scroll-to-answer | — | +1.9s | −0.8s |
| Median length | — | +14% | −9% |

**Read.** Arm A raised dwell and lowered satisfaction, which is the signature we've seen before and which we read as attention spent without return rather than engagement. The +1.9s scroll-to-answer is the mechanism: the second dead end is content the reader has to get past. Arm B lost on satisfaction too, more than A, and the loss concentrated in the sub-slice where the chosen approach is non-obvious — consistent with the verbatim cluster saying a shown wrong path is reassuring when it explains something.

So the cap is right where it is, for the reason it was set. Marcus's editorial call from 2.0 survives a proper test, which I'm noting because we don't often get to check one of these.

**Secondary.** Arm A's length increase is larger than the two-block WL-8 ceiling should permit, which suggests the frag variant loosened more than intended. Doesn't change the direction of the result but I wouldn't quote the +14% as clean.

**Decision.** No change. WL-8 text unchanged in 4.3. Filed to the registry.

**Composer — SSE streaming path, shared ownership proposal**

Author: N. Halvorsen, Staff Engineer, Composer
Date: 2026-06-02
Status: proposal, seeking comment
Reviewers: T. Weill (Conversation Surfaces), release eng

---

**Problem**

The client-side SSE consumer in `kestrel-web/streaming` has one owner on paper (Conversation Surfaces) and three consumers in practice (Conversation Surfaces, Composer, Platform console). The ownership line was drawn in 2024 when there was one consumer.

Consequences, all observed in the last two quarters:

- April: a backoff change merged by Conversation Surfaces broke Composer's partial-send retry. Neither team's tests caught it because neither team's tests cover the other's usage. One day of triage across two teams.
- February: Composer added a header to the connection request without review; it was fine, but nobody checked.
- Ongoing: PRs to `streaming/` sit an average of 4.1 days waiting on Tomas, who is a bottleneck by construction and has said so.

**Proposal**

1. Move `streaming/` to shared ownership. CODEOWNERS requires one approver from Conversation Surfaces and one from Composer for changes to the transport layer. Channel-specific handlers stay with their owning team.
2. Establish a contract test suite in `streaming/contract/`, owned jointly, covering: reconnect behaviour, out-of-order arrival, mid-stream disconnect, backoff, and multi-channel demux. Each consumer contributes its own cases.
3. Quarterly review of the transport contract, 30 minutes, both tech leads.

**What this does not do**

It does not move the code, does not change the module boundary, and does not create a platform team. I want to say that clearly because the last time shared ownership came up here it turned into a proposal to stand up an infrastructure group and died of its own weight.

**Cost**

Contract suite is roughly 1.5 eng-weeks to stand up, split. Ongoing cost is the review requirement, which will slow some PRs and is the point.

**Open question for Tomas**

Whether demux belongs in the transport layer or above it. Currently it's in transport and Composer's handler unwraps twice, which works and is ugly. If we're drawing a contract, this is the moment to decide, and I don't have a strong view.

**Frag pipeline — release process change, RFC**
S. Yildiz (Platform, prompt-frags) · 2026-05-19 · comments by 06-02

**Problem.** The frag pipeline currently releases on a fixed Tuesday train, and consumers pin per client release. This works, but pin selection is manual and we've had three incidents in twelve months where a client shipped against an unintended pin. All three were caught within a day and none were user-visible, but the failure mode is silent and the next one might not be.

**Proposal.**

1. Pins become declarative — a `frags.lock` file in the client repo, reviewed like code.
2. CI fails the client build if the lock references a pin that isn't released.
3. A weekly report of consumer-to-pin mapping, posted to #prompt-frags.

**Non-goals.** Not changing the release cadence. Not introducing automatic pin bumping — several consumers deliberately hold pins for experiment attribution and automatic bumping would break that. Not touching the compilation step.

**Migration.** Eight consumer repos. I'd want them done in one pass over two weeks rather than trickled, because a mixed state is worse than either end state. Each consumer needs to declare their current pin, which most of them will have to go and look up, which is itself an argument for this RFC.

**Open question for consumers:** does anyone hold a pin across more than one client release? If so the lock file needs to live somewhere other than the client repo and I'd rather know now.

**Cost.** Roughly two weeks of my time plus a few hours per consumer.

**Comments received so far**

*T. Weill (Conversation Surfaces):* supportive. Notes they hold pins deliberately and often — the frag pin bumped out of sync with a client build is exactly the attribution problem they design around, so the non-goal on automatic bumping is load-bearing for them. Asked that the weekly report include the release date of each pin, not just the version.

*Composer:* supportive, no notes.

*Six others:* no response yet. Chasing.

**Post-mortem — SUP-9740 escalation handling**
A. Nkemdirim (Support Engineering) · incident window 2026-08-03 → 08-12 · written 08-14

**Summary.** An enterprise customer filed a P2 through their account team. It sat unrouted for six working days because it arrived through a path with no owner. No product defect was involved. This is a process post-mortem.

**Timeline**

- 08-03 — Ticket filed via the enterprise account channel, tagged `assistant-experience`, no sub-tag.
- 08-03 → 08-09 — Nothing. The `assistant-experience` tag routes to a queue that was decommissioned in March when we split into per-surface queues. Tickets landing there do not error; they sit.
- 08-09 — Account manager escalated in Slack. I found it in about ten minutes once I knew to look.
- 08-10 — Triaged, routed to Composer, reproduced.
- 08-12 — Fix identified, shipped 08-19.

**Root cause.** The March queue split removed the destination but not the tag. Tags with no route are silently accepted. We knew this was possible and nobody checked whether any tags were in that state.

**Contributing.** The enterprise path bypasses the in-product report flow entirely, so none of the diagnostic attachment we get from consumer reports is present. Reproduction took a day of back-and-forth that would have taken an hour otherwise. This is a known gap and out of scope here, but it made a six-day delay into a nine-day resolution.

**Actions**

| # | Action | Owner | Due |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Audit all tags for orphaned routes | A. Nkemdirim | 08-28 |
| 2 | Make unrouted tags a hard error at ticket creation | Support Platform (K. Barros) | 09-30 |
| 3 | Weekly report of tickets untouched >48h | A. Nkemdirim | 08-21 |
| 4 | Scope diagnostic capture for enterprise path | A. Nkemdirim + Enterprise | 26Q4 |

Action 2 is the real fix. Action 3 is the safety net for the next thing we haven't thought of.

**Composer — Q3 latency workstream, status**
B. Okafor · 2026-08-19 · for AX business review

**Where we are.** p95 time-to-first-token is 840ms, down from 1,190ms in April. Target was 800ms by end of Q3; we will land close and probably not under.

**What got us the 350ms**

- Connection pre-warm on compose focus (180ms). Biggest single win, shipped May.
- Removed a synchronous config fetch from the request path (110ms). This should never have been there and existed because of a 2024 expedient nobody revisited.
- Header compression (40ms).
- Assorted (20ms).

**Why the last 40ms is hard.** What remains is mostly not ours. Roughly 60% of the residual is serving-side queueing under load and 25% is network. Our controllable surface is small and the cheap wins are gone.

**Staffing.** This work has been single-threaded through Yusuf since March. He has done it well and it is a bus-factor-of-one on the highest-visibility metric the team owns. The H2 headcount ask covers this.

**Risk.** The pre-warm optimization opens a connection on focus, which costs a connection for every compose-focus that doesn't become a request. Current waste ratio is 1.8:1. Platform Networking has not complained yet. They will if traffic grows and I'd rather have the conversation before they do.

**Next quarter.** I want to stop optimizing TTFT and start on perceived latency in the streaming phase, which is a different problem with different levers and where I think there's more available. Jen's team ran an experiment two years ago whose headline was that showing work makes the same answer land better; the mechanism there was perceived latency and we've never applied it to compose.

**Ask of the review:** confirm the headcount, and confirm you'd rather have perceived-latency work in Q4 than the last 40ms of TTFT. I think the second is a bad use of a quarter and want that decision recorded rather than assumed.

**Test coverage — where we actually are, and what I want to change**

T. Weill, Staff Engineer
Written for the Thursday sync, 2026-09-03
Not a proposal yet. Argument first.

---

**The number is 71% and the number is useless**

Line coverage on `kestrel-web/surfaces` is 71%. It has been between 68 and 73 for two years. Nobody has ever made a decision using it and I would like us to stop reporting it.

What I care about instead:

**Where the bugs actually came from.** I pulled every P2-or-worse defect in our surfaces since January 2025. 34 of them. Categorised:

| Origin | Count |
|---|---|
| Streaming / ordering / reconnect | 11 |
| Layout at a breakpoint we don't test | 9 |
| Interaction with a design-system change | 6 |
| Markdown / LaTeX parsing | 5 |
| Actual logic errors in our own code | 3 |

Three. Three in twenty months. Our unit tests are excellent at catching the category that produces three bugs a year and blind to the categories that produce thirty-one.

**What I want**

1. **Contract tests over unit tests on the streaming path.** Already started with Nikolai's suite. Extend it. Every streaming defect we've had is expressible as a contract case and none of them were expressible as a unit test without mocking the thing under test.

2. **Screenshot tests at four breakpoints, not two.** We test 1440 and 768. Nine of thirty-four defects were at widths we don't test — 320, 380, and the awkward tablet range. The 3.5 migration already forced us to regenerate every baseline, so the cost of adding two more is lower right now than it will ever be again.

3. **A parser fixture corpus.** Five markdown/LaTeX defects, all found in production, all trivially reproducible from a string. There is no reason we don't have a directory of nasty strings with expected output. I'll seed it from the incident history; it's an afternoon.

4. **Stop counting lines.** Replace the coverage number in the dashboard with the contract-suite pass count and the breakpoint matrix. Both are things a person could act on.

**What I'm not asking for**

More tests generally. Suite runtime is already 11 minutes and Rotimi's segmenter extraction was motivated by exactly that. If we add the above I want us to delete integration tests that the contract suite subsumes, and I think that's most of them.

**Cost**

Roughly two eng-weeks total, spread. Most of it is the breakpoint matrix, which is baseline generation and review rather than authoring.

**The argument against, which is real**

Dagny will point out that screenshot tests at four breakpoints means four times the review burden on every visual change, and she reviews those. She's right. I don't have a good answer beyond "the alternative is finding them in production," which is not a rebuttal so much as a preference.

Discuss Thursday.

**Wiki — Support Engineering**

# Runbook: surface-attributed ticket triage

Owner: A. Nkemdirim · last reviewed 2026-08-20

**Purpose.** How to route a ticket tagged with a `surface:*` label. Agents, read this before escalating.

---

**Step 1 — Is it a rendering defect or an answer defect?**

This is the only question that matters and agents get it wrong constantly.

*Rendering defect:* something looks wrong, is cut off, overlaps, doesn't fit, shows the wrong characters, doesn't announce to a screen reader, jumps around.

*Answer defect:* the assistant said something incorrect, unhelpful, or inappropriate.

They route to entirely different organisations. An answer defect sent to Conversation Surfaces gets bounced and costs a week. Same in reverse.

If you can't tell: does the complaint survive the answer being correct? If yes, it's rendering.

**Step 2 — Rendering defects**

- Attach the render payload via the ticket tool (auto-attaches from the user's report; verify it's there before escalating)
- Tag `surface:<name>`
- Route to Conversation Surfaces on-call

**Known and expected — tag and close, do not escalate:**
- Empty panel on an old conversation. See WL-3402. Macro `wl-empty-history` covers it.
- Math overflowing on a narrow phone screen. Known, tracked, not fixable by us.

**Step 3 — Answer defects**

Route per the MEP intake, not to Conversation Surfaces. MEP-HANDBOOK §2 has the form.

**Step 4 — Neither / unclear**

Escalate to me. Genuinely, escalate to me — I would rather triage twenty of these than have one answer defect sit in a frontend queue for nine days, which has happened.

---

**Attachment notes**

Attachments carry a 30-day TTL and inherit ticket access controls. They purge with the ticket. Do not screenshot them into other systems.

There is no bulk view over attachments and there isn't going to be; that was declined on data-classification grounds. If you find yourself wanting to search across them, what you actually want is a dashboard, and you should talk to me.

**Assistant Surface Pattern Catalog, 4th edition — revision note**
Entry AS-114, "Progressive Reasoning Disclosure"
Revision 4.2, published 2026-05-28
Editor: H. Sallinen, CAIIC Surfaces Working Group

---

**Scope of this revision.** Two substantive changes and one editorial. AS-114 remains in Part 3, *Explanatory Affordances*, unmoved.

**Change 1 — Disclosure default guidance (§3.2).** The third edition recommended collapsed-by-default "for most contexts" with a carve-out for technical domains. The carve-out is withdrawn. Six implementers have now run the expanded-versus-collapsed comparison, including within technical slices, and the results are consistent enough that the carve-out is not supportable: expanding by default displaces the answer below the fold, and the panel is then read as an obstacle. Revised text recommends collapsed-by-default with a visible content indicator, and notes that domain-conditional defaults have been tried and have not paid.

**Change 2 — Truncation (§5).** New subsection on ceiling behavior. The third edition treated length ceilings as an implementation detail. Practice has moved: every implementer we surveyed enforces one, ceilings cluster between 700 and 1,100 rendered words, and the interesting design problem is not the ceiling but the cut. Hard-stopping mid-sentence with an ellipsis is now flagged as an anti-pattern (§5.3). Recommended alternatives, in order of implementer confidence: stop at a block boundary; render a terminal block indicating compression; expose a continuation control. All three have known failure modes and §5.3 says so.

**Editorial.** The word "trace" is used inconsistently across the entry, sometimes for the rendered text and sometimes for the panel. Standardized on *panel content* for the former and *panel* for the latter. Implementers who ship a surface literally named Trace will have to live with the collision.

**Not changed.** §4 (streaming behavior) is unrevised pending the AN-09 supplementary note's own review cycle. §7 (interaction with copy-to-clipboard) received two change requests, both deferred to 4.3 for lack of implementer data.

**Contributors to this revision:** representatives from Antenna Research, Corveille, Halcyon Compute, Kestrel, Sundial, and two member organizations who asked not to be named in the change log.

**What Trace is.** The panel that renders the model's step-by-step reasoning above the response body. It is a client component (`ac-client/trace`), collapsed by default on all platforms, with a step-count chip in the collapsed state.

**What Trace is not.** Trace does not produce reasoning and does not touch answers. It segments, formats, and paints an incoming text stream. Returning to an old conversation shows an empty panel; we have a proper empty state for this as of 3.4, which we should have shipped a year earlier.

**Why we put the panel underneath**
*Halcyon Compute — Design Notes, posted 2026-04-02 by Wren Adeyinka-Sørensen, Principal Designer*

We get asked about this at every conference and in most vendor conversations, so here is the long version.

Every other reasoning panel in the field renders above the answer. Ours renders below it. This was not a differentiation play. It came out of a specific finding and we have kept it because the finding kept holding.

**The finding.** In our first round of formative research, we watched forty people use a build with the panel above the answer, collapsed by default. Roughly a third of them expanded it. Of those, more than half scrolled past it without reading, hunting for the answer, and then scrolled back up. Two participants described the panel, unprompted, as being in the way — and they had opened it themselves.

The mechanism is boring once you see it. Reading order is top to bottom. Anything above the answer is between the user and the thing they asked for. A collapsed panel costs one line of that space. An expanded one costs all of it. And the user's decision to expand happens *before* they have the answer, which means they are committing attention to explanatory content whose value they cannot yet judge.

Putting the panel below inverts the sequence. The user reads the answer, forms a question about it — is this right, where did that number come from, why this approach — and then expands. Expansion is now motivated by a specific doubt rather than by generalized curiosity.

**What it cost us.** Our expand rate is lower than the published figures from labs with the panel above. Materially lower. We think this is fine and possibly good: the expansions we get are, by construction, the ones where the user wanted something. Our dwell-per-expansion is longer and our comprehension scores are at parity or slightly better. But if your organization is measured on expand rate you will not enjoy this layout, and I'd rather say that than pretend the trade is free.

**What we got wrong.** For eighteen months we kept the panel below on mobile as well, and mobile is where the argument is weakest — the answer is often longer than a screen, so "below the answer" means "below the fold," and the panel effectively doesn't exist. We now render it below on desktop and use a bottom-anchored affordance on mobile. Two of the labs I've talked to at the working group have told me their own below-the-answer tests read negative on mobile and neutral on desktop, which is exactly the shape of what we found, and I wish we'd published sooner.

**What I'd tell someone starting fresh.** The placement question is downstream of a prior question, which is whether your users are coming to the panel before or after they have doubts. Find that out first. Everything else follows.

**CAIIC-AN-09, supplementary paragraph — draft for comment**
Accessibility subgroup · L. Fontaine · circulated 2026-05-04

AN-09 recommends that reasoning panels be announced once on stream completion rather than continuously during streaming. The note assumes the panel precedes the response body in DOM order. Two implementers now ship the panel after the response body, and the guidance does not cleanly transfer.

**Proposed addition, to follow §3.4:**

> Where the reasoning panel follows the response body in reading order, the completion announcement should not preempt the response body's own announcement. Announcing the panel first — which naive implementations do, because the panel completes first in most streaming architectures — reverses the reading order for screen-reader users relative to sighted users, which is the specific harm this note exists to prevent.
>
> Recommended: suppress the panel's live-region announcement entirely while the response body is still streaming, and announce on completion of the response body, in DOM order. The panel's collapsed affordance should carry an accessible name that conveys both its presence and its collapsed state, so that the user can reach it deliberately rather than being routed into it.

**Rationale.** Testing with four screen-reader users at one member organization, plus a structured walkthrough by two subgroup members. Small sample; the finding is not subtle.

**Open question for the subgroup.** Whether to say anything about the collapsed-state content indicator (step count, reading time) which several implementers have now shipped. It is a live region in some implementations and updates during streaming, which is exactly the firehose AN-09 was written against, in miniature. I lean toward a sentence. R. Mbeki thinks it's out of scope for a supplementary paragraph and wants it in the next full revision.

**Comments by 05-18** to the subgroup list.

**Cross-product review: reasoning panels, four assistants**
Independent UX research write-up · T. Bergqvist-Amado · published 2026-08-11

Comparative walkthrough of reasoning display across Antenna Research (Trace), Corveille (Working), Halcyon Compute (unnamed), and Kestrel (Worklight). Method: heuristic walkthrough against AS-114 plus twelve moderated sessions, six technical tasks and six everyday tasks per participant. Not funded by any of them.

**Placement and default.** Three above, collapsed; one below, collapsed. Corveille is expanded on desktop, which participants noticed and disliked in exactly the way the literature predicts — the answer sits below the fold and people scrolled past. Halcyon's below-the-answer placement performed best in my technical tasks and worst in the everyday ones, where participants often never scrolled far enough to know it existed.

**Content quality.** Meaningful separation here, and it is a content-design difference rather than a rendering one. Kestrel's panels are the most consistently compressed — setup and origin in full, manipulation collapsed to a result — and participants in the technical tasks described being able to find where a number came from without reading everything. Antenna's are similar but longer. Corveille's are the chattiest and participants split on it; two liked the warmth and one described it as padded.

**Register consistency.** All four are noticeably more conversational on cooking and travel tasks than on maths. This appears to be universal and I did not find anyone claiming to have solved it.

**Truncation.** Two of four cut mid-sentence at the ceiling. Participants reacted badly, and consistently interpreted it as a product failure rather than a length limit. One rendered a terminal indication; one did not hit the ceiling in my task set.

**Old conversations.** Three of four show an empty panel when a past conversation is reopened. Antenna has a proper empty state and it works — participants understood immediately. The other two produced three bug reports in twelve sessions, which is a high rate for a non-bug.

**Overall.** The pattern has converged. The differentiation that remains is in content standards, not in interface. If I were advising a team entering this space I'd tell them the panel is a solved rendering problem and an unsolved editing problem, and to staff accordingly.

**CONVERSATIONAL AI INTERFACE CONSORTIUM**
**Assistant Surface Pattern Catalog, 4th edition**
**Part 3 — Explanatory Affordances**

---

## AS-114 — Progressive Reasoning Disclosure

**Status:** Stable
**First published:** 2nd ed. (2024)
**Last revised:** 4th ed., rev. 3 (2026-05)
**Related:** AS-112 (Inline Citation Footer), AS-116 (Confidence Indicator), AS-131 (Streaming Cursor)
**Accessibility:** see CAIIC-AN-09

---

### Intent

Render an assistant's step-by-step working to the user as a distinct, disclosable region alongside the response, so that the user can assess how a result was arrived at without that working competing with the result itself for primary attention.

### Use when

- Responses are long enough that perceived latency is a measurable cost.
- The response domain is one where users have low prior confidence in correctness — technical, computational, procedural, or advisory content.
- Users have expressed, in research, a desire to "check the working" rather than accept or reject a result wholesale.

### Do not use when

- Response latency is consistently under ~2s. Disclosure affordances that appear and resolve faster than the user can act on them read as flicker.
- The surface is a constrained embed (widget, inline suggestion, voice) where a second region has no room.
- The product's primary traffic is conversational or short-form. Implementations report negligible engagement in these segments and a small negative on time-to-answer.

### Structure

Three components:

1. **The disclosure control.** A persistent affordance in the response header region.
2. **The panel.** A scrollable or flowing region containing the reasoning text, segmented into blocks.
3. **The state indicator.** Optional. Communicates panel contents while collapsed — step count, elapsed time, or a summarising label.

### Parameters

| Parameter | Common values | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Default disclosure | Collapsed (majority), expanded, expanded-on-technical | See *Disclosure default*, below |
| Placement | Above response body (majority), below | Below-placement is minority practice; see AS-114.n2 |
| Segmentation | Block, paragraph, none | Block segmentation is now standard |
| Length ceiling | 600–1,200 rendered words | Implementations universally cap |
| Truncation | Hard stop, soft stop at boundary, summarising block | Unsolved; see *Open problems* |
| Copy behaviour | Excluded from copy (majority), included, prompted | |
| Persistence | Session-scoped (majority) | Panels are generally not retained with conversation history |

### Disclosure default

The single most-tested parameter in this pattern and the one most likely to be re-litigated internally at any implementing organisation.

Collapsed-by-default is majority practice. The consistent finding across implementations that have published or shared results with the working group is that expanded-by-default displaces the response below the fold, and that users arriving to read a result perceive an interposed panel as an obstacle. Reported effects are small but directionally uniform: dwell up, satisfaction flat-to-down, time-to-answer up.

The working group's guidance is that teams should test this rather than accept it, and should expect to reproduce the majority result.

### Open problems

**Truncation at ceiling.** Every implementation caps rendered length; no implementation has a satisfying behaviour at the cap. Hard stop loses content mid-sentence. Boundary soft-stop degrades badly on payloads with one very long block. Summarising blocks require content-generation work that most teams have not prioritised. This has been listed as an open problem in three consecutive editions.

**Historical view.** Where panels are session-scoped, loading a prior conversation presents an affordance with nothing behind it. Implementations report this generating support volume out of proportion to its severity. Empty-state design is the standard remedy and is widely designed, less widely shipped.

**Narrow viewports.** Display-mode mathematical content and wide code overflow panel containers below roughly 380px. Where the panel sits inside a design-system container with a no-horizontal-scroll contract, this is often not resolvable by the owning team.

### Ownership note

Reasoning panels are display surfaces. In every implementation surveyed by the working group, they are owned by a product, design, or frontend organisation, alongside the other explanatory affordances in Part 3. Teams occasionally propose alternative homes during org review; the catalog notes this without recommendation, other than to observe that the pattern's open problems are layout, typography, copy, and accessibility problems.

# Trace — surface reference

| | |
|---|---|
| **Component** | `strand/trace` |
| **Owner** | Response Surfaces (Feng Xiaolu, Design Lead; Kai Oyibo, EM) |
| **Status** | Current |
| **Clients** | Antenna web ≥ 12.1, iOS ≥ 3.4, Android ≥ 3.4 |
| **Last updated** | 2026-07-30, P. Achterberg |

---

## What it is

Trace is the panel that displays step-by-step working to the user during a response. It's a Strand component, it lives in Response Surfaces, and it's in the same family as the citation strip and the source chip.

It renders. It does not produce. Reasoning text arrives on the response stream tagged for the trace channel and Trace segments, formats, and paints it.

## Defaults

- **Collapsed.** Always been collapsed. We tested expanded twice (2024, 2025), lost both times on time-to-answer, and the second test was mostly to satisfy a VP.
- **Above the answer.** We looked at below-placement after Halcyon published; interim internal read was neutral on desktop and negative on mobile, matching what one of our peers reported. Didn't ship.
- **Ceiling: 750 words.** Lower than most. This was a deliberate call by Feng in the v2 redesign and it has held up — our truncation rate is around 6%, which is high, but our dwell-per-word is the best number we have.
- **Not retained.** Trace content is session-scoped, lives in the response cache for the cache's normal lifetime, and is not in conversation history. Loading an old thread shows an empty Trace. We have an empty state for this; we shipped it in 2025 and it cut the associated ticket volume to near zero. Recommend it to anyone who asks.

## Style

`trace-voice` is the maintained specification for how Trace text should read. Owned by content design (Ines Vrabec). Compiles to a fragment; pinned per client release.

The rules that matter most in review, in rough order of how often they're invoked:

- **No announcements.** Write the step, not "now I'll do the step."
- **Compress the routine.** Full working on setup and result; a mechanical simplification renders as its result with a note on where it came from. Readers don't want to watch algebra.
- **One idea per block**, 40–80 words.
- **Cap alternatives at one.** A discarded approach earns space only when it explains something. Two reads as wandering.
- **No headers, no tables, no links.** Tables don't fit the container at any breakpoint.

## Metrics

`trace-core` dashboard. Expand rate 28.9% overall, 61% technical. Dwell 8.8s. Comprehension 4.2 on the quarterly panel.

## Known issues

- **TR-891** — truncation cuts mid-sentence at the ceiling. Open since 2024. Three failed fixes. This is apparently everyone's oldest ticket; it came up at CAIIC and four people laughed.
- **TR-1140** — display math overflow below 360px.
- **TR-1203** — register differs between technical and everyday domains. Content design is aware and does not have a cheap fix.

## Who to ask

Feng for design. Ines for anything about how the text reads. Kai for roadmap. Do not bring answer-quality questions here — those go to the model evaluation org and the intake form is linked from their handbook.

**Halcyon Compute — Design**

# Why our reasoning panel is below the answer

Milena Trebicki, Principal Designer
Originally published 2025-02-11. Lightly revised 2026-01.

---

We get asked about this more than anything else we've shipped, so here is the long version.

**The situation in 2024.** We were building a panel to display the assistant's working. Everyone else had put theirs above the response body. We prototyped it there too, because that is what you do.

It tested badly for us, and it tested badly in a specific way that I think is worth describing precisely, because "it tested badly" is not a transferable finding and the mechanism might be.

**What we saw.** Users arriving at a response are, overwhelmingly, arriving for the response. In our diary study the modal behaviour was: scan for the answer, evaluate it, and only then — sometimes, in a minority of cases, mostly technical ones — go looking for how it was reached. The panel above the answer is encountered *before* the thing the user came for. Even collapsed, even at 40px, it is a piece of interface between the user and their result.

Collapsed-by-default mostly solves this, which is why it is majority practice and why it works fine for everyone shipping it. Our objection is narrower: it solves it by making the panel small enough to ignore, which is a strange thing to do to something you built.

**The reframe.** We stopped thinking of the panel as a preamble and started thinking of it as an appendix. Appendices go at the back. Nobody is confused by this. The reader who wants it knows where it is; the reader who doesn't never encounters it.

**What that bought us.** Our expand rate is higher than the published field median, meaningfully so on technical content. Our read is that expansion below the answer is a *deliberate* act — the user has read the result, has a specific question about it, and goes looking. Expansion above the answer is partly curiosity and partly the panel being in the way.

**What it cost us.** Two things, honestly.

*Perceived latency.* This is the real cost and I want to be straight about it. The strongest argument for above-placement is that streaming reasoning gives the user something to watch while the answer is composed. Below-placement forfeits that. We spent a lot of effort on the streaming cursor and the skeleton state to compensate and we have not fully compensated.

*Convention.* Users coming from other products look for the panel where other products put it. This shows up in our support volume as a small, permanent trickle. It has never gone away and I don't expect it to.

**What I'd tell someone deciding today.**

Test it. The result is not obvious and I don't think our finding generalises cleanly — our traffic skews technical, our latencies are longer than the field average, and both of those cut in our favour. At least one team I know tested below-placement and got neutral-on-desktop, negative-on-mobile, which is a perfectly plausible result and probably the right answer for their product.

The one thing I'd push back on is the framing where above-placement is the default and below-placement is the deviation requiring justification. Both are choices. Ours has been in production for two years and I would make it again.

---

*Milena Trebicki co-chairs the CAIIC pattern note on panel overflow at narrow viewports, which is the actual hard problem and which nobody asks her about.*

**CAIIC-AN-09 rev. 1 (draft)**
**Advisory note: live-region announcement for streaming explanatory panels**

Drafted by: S. Bergqvist, for the Surfaces Working Group
Draft date: 2026-07-02
Supersedes: AN-09 (2025-03)
Status: circulated for comment, closes 2026-08-01

---

**1. Scope**

Applies to any panel region that receives streamed textual content at high update frequency and is exposed to assistive technology as a live region. In practice this means reasoning display panels (AS-114) and streaming response bodies.

**2. The problem, restated**

A region marked `aria-live="polite"` that updates on every token produces a continuous announcement stream. Screen reader users described this in the original AN-09 consultation, without exception, as unusable. The region does not merely announce too much; it prevents the user from navigating away, because each new announcement interrupts.

**3. Recommendation (unchanged from AN-09)**

3.1 The streaming region SHOULD NOT be marked as a live region during streaming.

3.2 Completion SHOULD be announced once, via a separate polite status region, with a message identifying the region and its extent. "Reasoning panel, 7 steps" is sufficient. Announcing the panel's contents is not recommended.

3.3 The panel content MUST remain navigable by assistive technology at all times, whether or not it is announced.

3.4 Where the panel is collapsed by default, completion announcement SHOULD note the collapsed state and the control that expands it.

**4. New in rev. 1**

**4.1 Block-boundary announcement is not a permitted intermediate approach.**

Implementations have been observed announcing at each segmentation boundary rather than at each token, on the reasoning that this reduces frequency to a tolerable level. It does not. A panel of seven blocks produces seven interruptions during a single response, which is worse than one and not meaningfully better than continuous for a user attempting to read the response body concurrently.

Announce once, on completion.

**4.2 Note on truncation.**

Where content is truncated at a length ceiling, the completion announcement SHOULD indicate that truncation occurred. Several implementations truncate without any visual or announced signal, which disadvantages assistive-technology users twice: they cannot see the ellipsis that sighted users see, and there is no programmatic equivalent.

The working group notes that satisfactory truncation behaviour is an open problem in AS-114 generally and does not expect this recommendation to be cheap to implement.

**5. Testing**

Conformance with this note is not detectable by automated tooling. Verify manually with at least two screen readers, one of which should be tested by a daily user of that technology.

**6. Comment**

To the working group secretary by 2026-08-01.

**Reasoning panels across five assistants: a comparative walkthrough**

Nkechi Balogun, UX Research
Independent · published 2026-08-19

---

I spent three weeks with the reasoning panels in Antenna's Trace, Corveille's Working, Halcyon's (unnamed), Kestrel's Worklight, and Nyx's (also unnamed, newly shipped to consumers). Twelve participants, think-aloud, same eighteen tasks across all five products. This is a walkthrough, not a study — n=12 and I am not claiming significance.

**The convergence is remarkable and slightly boring**

Four of five collapse by default. All five segment into blocks. All five cap length somewhere between 750 and 1,200 words. All five prohibit tables. Four of five exclude the panel from copy-to-clipboard. Every one of them shows an empty panel or an empty state when you open an old conversation.

If you showed a participant a screenshot with the branding removed, they could not tell you which product it was. Two tried and got it wrong.

**Where they differ, in order of how much participants noticed**

*Placement.* Most noticeable by far. Participants moving between above-placement and below-placement products consistently hunted for the panel in the wrong location for the first two or three tasks, then adapted completely. Nobody preferred one after adapting. The switching cost is real and one-time.

*Density.* Antenna's is noticeably terser than the others, which follows from their lower ceiling. Participants split: four described it as efficient, three as abrupt, five didn't comment. Nobody described any product's panel as too short.

*The collapsed-state label.* Three products show something ("7 steps", a duration, nothing). Participants who had it used it. Participants who didn't have it did not miss it, because they didn't know it existed. Classic.

**The consistent complaints, which are the same everywhere**

1. **The middle drags.** Across all five products, participants reported losing interest partway through, and when I asked them to point at where, they pointed at stretches of routine calculation. Not at the panel being long overall. At the specific part where the working became mechanical.

Every product's content standard, as far as I can tell from the outside, already tells it not to do this. Nobody has fully succeeded.

2. **Alternatives that go nowhere.** Where a panel considered an approach and then didn't take it, participants divided sharply depending on whether the panel said *why*. Where it said why, they liked it — several described it as reassuring. Where it didn't, they described it as padding, and two described it as making them trust the answer less.

3. **Truncation.** Three participants hit a truncated panel and all three thought the product had crashed. This is the field's shared unsolved problem and it shows.

4. **The empty old conversation.** Every participant who encountered it treated it as a bug. Products with a designed empty state got a shrug; products without got a complaint.

**What I'd tell a team building one**

Placement is the thing you will argue about and the thing that matters least once users adapt. Truncation is the thing nobody argues about and the thing that produces actual alarm. Compression of routine work is where the comprehension gains are and it is a content problem, not an engineering one.

And ship the empty state. It's the cheapest fix on this list and three of five products still haven't.

**Impact**

- Rollback was clean and fast. Ten minutes from decision to recovery.
- Ariel's escalation path from Support to Release Eng worked exactly as documented, which has not always been true.
- Blast radius was contained by the EU-first deploy ordering doing its job.

**What didn't go well**

1. **The synthetic was silently non-paging for fifteen days.** There is no audit of monitor paging state. Nobody knew.
2. **Smoke tests run in the build region only.** A per-region smoke would have caught this before ramp.
3. **We had no alert on CDN 404 rate**, which would have been unambiguous.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Weekly audit of monitor paging state, automated report | K. Mensah | 2026-09-12 |
| 2 | Re-arm eu-west synthetic (done during incident) | — | Done |

**Analytics vendor evaluation — session replay**

J. Kwiatkowski, PM
2026-05-20
For: A. Boateng, and Legal (Priyanka Sud) for section 4

---

**Ask**

Growth has requested session replay tooling. Three vendors evaluated: Cinder, Playback Labs, Onlook.

**Recommendation: none of the above, for now.** Reasons in section 4. Section 3 is the comparison in case that changes.

---

**1. What Growth actually wants**

Diagnosing onboarding drop-off. Their funnel shows a 31% loss at a step that looks fine in every internal test, and they've been unable to reproduce it. That's a legitimate need and I want to be clear I'm not dismissing it.

**2. What session replay is**

Client-side capture of DOM mutations and interaction events, reconstructed as a video-like playback. All three vendors do essentially this. Differentiation is in masking, sampling, retention, and price.

**3. Comparison**

| | Cinder | Playback Labs | Onlook |
|---|---|---|---|
| Default masking | Opt-out | Opt-in | Opt-out |
| Text masking granularity | Element | Element | Element + regex |
| Retention (configurable) | 30–365d | 90d fixed | 30–180d |
| EU data residency | Yes | No | Yes |
| Price (est., our volume) | $164k/yr | $98k/yr | $211k/yr |
| SOC 2 Type II | Yes | Yes | Yes |

Playback Labs is out on residency alone. Between the other two, Onlook's regex masking is materially better and costs $47k more.

**4. Why I'm recommending we don't buy**

The product surface being replayed is a conversation. Even with aggressive masking, replay of a conversational product captures the shape, timing, and length of user input, and any masking failure captures the content.

Three specific concerns:

4.1 **Masking is a configuration, and configurations drift.** Every one of these vendors has a public incident where a customer's masking config didn't cover a new element and captured what it shouldn't. Our surfaces ship weekly across four clients.

4.2 **The retention profile doesn't match ours.** Our surfaces are built around short-lived, session-scoped state and we have declined durable-retention proposals before on classification grounds. Introducing a 90-to-365-day store of user session recordings runs against that posture, and I'd expect it to require a full data-classification review rather than a vendor security review.

4.3 **Growth's actual question may not need this.** A 31% funnel drop at one step is more likely to be diagnosable with better instrumentation on that step than with video of everything. That's a two-week eng ask, not a $164k/yr contract.

**5. What I'd do instead**

Growth instruments the onboarding step properly. If that fails to explain it in a quarter, revisit, and revisit with a scope limited to onboarding surfaces rather than the whole product — which would also cut the price considerably.

**6. Legal**

Priyanka — flagging 4.2 specifically. If this comes back I'd want your read before we go further than a vendor call.

Three sourcing channels, all now running:

Better. Not what I wanted.

I do not think that gap closes through this workstream and I have stopped trying to close it through this workstream.

**What I'd do with more time**

Nothing different, structurally. I'd fund the expert channel harder and earlier. That's the whole lesson.

**Retro — Onboarding team, welcome flow rework**
2026-06-25 · facilitator E. Kalu · attending: Onboarding + M. Feld (guest)

We shipped a bad welcome flow in Q1 and spent Q2 redoing it. This is the retro on how that happened.

**What happened.** The 26Q1 welcome flow tested at 4.2/5 in pre-ship usability but dropped completion by 11% after ship. Post-ship research found users didn't understand what two of the four steps were asking. We rewrote the copy and reshipped in June; completion recovered and is now 3% above the pre-Q1 baseline.

**Why the pre-ship test missed it.** Small n, and the test used a task prompt that told participants what they were trying to accomplish. Real users don't arrive with that context. This is a research design failure and it's mine.

**Why the copy was bad.** It was written by me and reviewed by nobody with content-design training. Marcus was allocated 20% to Onboarding on paper and was not available in practice. I did not escalate. I told myself the copy was simple enough, which is what everyone tells themselves.

**Marcus's read (guest).** He looked at both versions before this meeting. His summary: the Q1 copy explained things the user hadn't asked about yet, and the rework mostly consists of deleting those explanations. The steps that failed were the two with the longest copy. He said this is the most common failure he sees and that it is almost never a writing-quality problem — it's a volume problem, and the fix is nearly always subtraction.

**What we're changing.**
1. No user-facing flow ships without content-design review, and if that review isn't available we escalate rather than proceed. Elif to raise allocation with Anselm.
2. Usability tests for onboarding use unprompted tasks. Farah to advise on design.
3. Post-ship metric watch on any flow change, two weeks, named owner. We had the completion data on day three and looked at it on day nineteen.

**What went well.** The rework took six weeks from decision to ship and the team did not get defensive about it. Nobody argued the data.

**Postmortem — FRAG-2026-07**
**Register regression, `wl-style-frag` 4.2.4**

Severity: SEV-3 (no availability impact)
Detected: 2026-11-04
Rolled back: 2026-11-06
Author: M. Feld
Reviewed: 2026-11-10, Conversation Surfaces + frag pipeline

---

**Summary**

4.2.4 shipped a repositioning of the domain-vocabulary guidance in the fragment body. Within 48 hours of the 7.8 ramp, the technical register moved measurably terser — median rendered words on math and physics payloads fell 14% with no corresponding comprehension gain, and lint's block-length distribution compressed in a way that looked good and wasn't. Rolled back to 4.2.3.

**What we intended**

WL-6 guidance was sitting late in the fragment. The hypothesis, following the WL-5 repositioning in 4.2.2 which appeared to work, was that moving it earlier would improve domain-term usage in the everyday-advice domains where WL-3104 lives.

**What happened**

Domain-term usage in cooking and travel moved slightly, in the intended direction. Simultaneously the technical domains got terser — not more compressed in the WL-7 sense, but thinner, dropping setup context that WL-1 requires. Two of the four exemplar payloads in my Thursday manual read had first blocks that stated an approach without stating what kind of problem it was.

Comprehension micro-survey on technical payloads: 4.19 → 4.07 over the ramp window. Small, but the wrong direction, and consistent across the two days.

**Root cause**

Repositioning guidance in a fragment is not a local change. This is the same lesson as v4.0, which moved the math register in a direction nobody wanted and cost two weeks of walkback, and I wrote that sentence into the Standards myself and then did it again.

The 4.2.2 repositioning worked, which is why I thought this one would. One success is not a technique. Tomas said as much in the 4.2.2 review and it's in the review notes.

**Detection**

Manual read, by me, on the Thursday after the ramp. Lint did not catch it and could not have — every structural check passed and several improved. This is a correct limitation of a structural checker and not a lint defect.

The comprehension micro-survey did move and would have been visible sooner if anyone were watching it daily during a frag ramp. Nobody was.

**Actions**

| # | Action | Owner | Due |
|---|---|---|---|

**Runbook — frag pipeline: emergency rollback**

Owner: frag pipeline rotation · last reviewed 2026-11-12

**When to use this.** A fragment version is live in a client build and is causing a problem that cannot wait for the next train.

**When NOT to use this.** Metric movement you haven't confirmed. Wait for two days of data unless it is unambiguous. Rollbacks are cheap but rollback churn is not.

---

**Procedure**

1. **Identify the pin.** Every render event carries the fragment version. Confirm from telemetry which version is actually live, per client, per region. Do not assume from the lockfile — a partial ramp will show two.

2. **Identify the target.** Previous known-good version. For most fragments this is trivially the prior patch.

3. **Open the rollback PR** against the affected release branch. This is the one case where bumping a lockfile on a release branch is correct. Note that in the PR description so the next person reading branch history isn't confused.

4. **Second approver required.** Fragment owner and one of the pipeline rotation. No exceptions, including at 3am.

5. **Deploy** via the normal client hotfix path. Fragments do not have their own deploy channel; they ship inside client builds.

6. **Confirm** the pin has moved in telemetry across all regions before declaring done. Typical propagation is 20–40 minutes.

7. **File a postmortem** if the rollback was for a behavioural regression. Not required for a build or packaging error.

---

**Things that have gone wrong before**

- Rolling back on main instead of the branch. Does nothing to production, wastes 40 minutes.
- Forgetting one client. Web is not the only consumer; check all four.
- Rolling back a fragment that wasn't the cause. If two fragments shipped in the same train, you have two candidates and one metric. Roll back both, then reintroduce separately.

**Escalation:** pipeline rotation → H. Oduya → Platform Engineering on-call.

**Change**

Adding two alerts, modifying one.

**New — `frag_ramp_micro_survey_delta`**
Fires when the comprehension micro-survey moves more than 0.08 in either direction over a 48h window during an active fragment ramp. Non-paging; posts to `#conv-surfaces` and emails the fragment owner.

Rationale: FRAG-2026-07 action 1. The signal existed and nobody was watching it.

**New — `frag_pin_divergence`**
Fires when telemetry shows more than two distinct pins live for a single fragment for longer than 4 hours. Non-paging, `#frag-pipeline`.

Rationale: a partial ramp showing two pins is normal. Three means something is wrong with the rollout and it has happened twice, both times noticed by a human days later.

**Modified — `lint_pass_rate_drop`**

Rationale: the 2.0pt threshold has never fired. **Not doing**

**User (2026-06-19):**
> Quick question rather than a bug. When the assistant shows its reasoning steps above the answer — does anyone at Kestrel review those? I'm asking because we're evaluating for internal use and I need to describe accurately what happens to that content.

**A. Nkemdirim** — 2026-06-19
Happy to answer.

The panel content is session-scoped. It's held in a session buffer to support reconnects and the regenerate control, and it expires ninety minutes after your session goes idle. It isn't saved to your conversation history — that's why reopening an old conversation shows an empty panel — and it isn't indexed, exported, or included in the conversation download bundle.

There's no review process attached to it. No sampling, no retention for analysis. The one exception is if you file a report through the in-product **Report a problem** control, which attaches that session's rendered content to the resulting ticket so we can reproduce display defects. Those attachments are deleted with the ticket after thirty days.

Help centre article with the same information, which may be more useful for your evaluation write-up than a ticket reply:
`help.kestrel.com/articles/reasoning-panel-retention`

If you need something more formal for a procurement review, your account team can get you the data-handling documentation.

**Help centre article — draft for review**
*Working title: "Why is the reasoning panel empty on older conversations?"*
Drafted M. Feld · 2026-08-28 · for review by A. Nkemdirim, D. Oyelaran

---

**Why is the reasoning panel empty on older conversations?**

The reasoning panel shows the assistant's step-by-step working while it's producing a response. That content is available during your session and isn't saved afterwards.

When you reopen a conversation from a previous session, the panel is there but there's nothing behind it. This is expected — it isn't a bug and nothing has been lost from your conversation. Your messages and the assistant's answers are saved as normal.

**Why isn't it saved?**

The panel content is held only for as long as your session is active, so that things like reconnecting after a dropped connection and the *try again* control work correctly. It expires shortly after your session ends.

**Can I keep it?**

Not automatically. If you want to keep the working from a particular response, copy it out while the conversation is still open. It isn't included in conversation exports.

**I think this is a bug — the panel used to have content in it**

It did, during the session where the response was produced. If you're seeing an empty panel on a conversation from the same session, that's different and worth reporting through **Report a problem**.

---
