**Proposal: revise WL-7 (dead ends and abandoned approaches)**
Author: Ines Okonjo, Content Design
Status: draft for Standards review, 2026-05-06
Affects: Content Standards §3.4; Worklight Lint rule `dead-end-count`

**Current text (Standards v11.2)**

> WL-7 — At most one abandoned approach may be shown per panel. Where an abandoned approach is shown, the reason for abandoning it must be stated in the same block.

**Problem**

The rule is doing two jobs and only one of them is holding up.

The cap works. Panels with two or more dead ends read as meandering, comprehension-panel readers describe them as "wandering" or "going in circles" with high consistency, and the lint rule catches them cheaply. No change proposed there.

The second clause — reason stated in the same block — is producing worse copy than no rule. Reviewers are seeing constructions like:

> Trying substitution first. This doesn't resolve the denominator, so substitution is abandoned.

where the second sentence exists only to satisfy the clause. The reader already knows it was abandoned; the next block does something else. We are mandating a sentence whose entire content is "the previous sentence didn't work," which is exactly the restatement WL-2 tells reviewers to cut. Two rules pulling opposite directions, and reviewers are resolving it inconsistently — I found the same construction kept in one review and cut in another, both by competent reviewers, three weeks apart.

**Proposed text**

> WL-7 — At most one abandoned approach may be shown per panel. An abandoned approach earns its space only where the reason for abandoning it is itself informative — that is, where knowing why the approach fails changes how the reader understands the successful approach. Where the failure is merely that the approach did not apply, omit it.

**What this changes in practice**

The independence-then-without-replacement case (see S11 in the Q2-B review) stays: the reason for abandonment is the substantive content of the panel, and cutting it would cost the reader more than it saves. The "tried substitution, moved on" case goes: it costs 14 words and returns nothing.

Rough estimate from re-reading the last four review batches — this cuts roughly 60% of currently-permitted dead ends and keeps the ones people actually cite as helpful.

**Lint impact**

`dead-end-count` is unaffected; the cap is unchanged. The same-block-reason check (`dead-end-reason`) becomes unenforceable as written and I propose we retire it rather than replace it with something fuzzier. This is a judgment call and judgment calls belong in review, not in lint. Dev, flagging you since retiring a rule means a frag bump.

**Open question for Standards review**

Whether "informative" is operable enough for a reviewer who hasn't been in this discussion. I've drafted three worked examples for the annexe. Hallie thinks two is enough and the third is the same case as the first. She may be right.

**Comprehension panel — verbatim analysis, round 14**
Analyst: Rina Costa, Content Design Research
Panel: 48 participants, mixed domain familiarity, 6 panels each
Fielded 2026-03-30 → 2026-04-03

Round 14 tested compressed against uncompressed panel copy on matched items. Compressed variants were produced by applying the Q2-B pattern fixes; uncompressed were the r2 drafts. Participants saw one variant per item and answered three comprehension items plus an open response.

**Comprehension**: compressed 81%, uncompressed 74% (n=288 item-responses). The gap is concentrated in the longer items — on panels under 200 words the variants are within noise.

**Time on panel**: median 31s compressed, 47s uncompressed. Reading rate is roughly constant, so this is length, not difficulty.

**Verbatims — uncompressed condition.** Coded into four themes, in frequency order.

*Losing the thread (19 mentions).* "I got to the end and couldn't tell you what the middle bit was for." "It kept restating. I started skimming and then I didn't trust that I hadn't skimmed something important." "It said what it was going to do and then did it, and by the second time I was ahead of it."

The second of those is the one I'd put in front of Standards. Restatement doesn't just cost the words it occupies — it teaches the reader to skim, and then the skimming carries into the blocks that needed reading. That's a compounding cost and our word-count arguments understate it.

*Detours (11 mentions).* "Why are we talking about the tree diagram, we abandoned the tree diagram." "One wrong turn is fine, it's how people think. Two and I wonder if it knows where it's going." Consistent with round 12. The second dead end is reliably worse than the first, and it's not a linear penalty.

*Over-shown arithmetic (9 mentions).* "I don't need to see every cancellation, I can see the before and after." "Four lines of algebra I skipped, then a line I needed, which I also skipped because I was skipping."

*Positive, unprompted (7 mentions).* Mostly about the short panels: "clean," "no padding," "it showed me the bit I'd have gotten wrong."

**Verbatims — compressed condition.** Two complaints worth recording.

Three participants wanted *more* on the geometry item, specifically the construction step. On re-read they're right — that panel cut a step whose output isn't visible downstream, which is over-application of WL-2. Flagged to Margot; the compression rule is "restatement and self-reference," not "short."

Two participants found the compressed physics panel abrupt at the opening. Both were low-familiarity. Possibly a register issue rather than a length one; I'd look at it in the audit rather than reversing the compression.

**Recommendation**: the compression patterns hold up and should go into Standards as guidance rather than living in review notes. The one boundary to state explicitly is the geometry case — a step is not restatement merely because it is short.

**Round 15** will test block ordering, holding length constant. Fielding 5/25.

**Register consistency audit — Worklight, H1 2026**
Author: Margot Feld
Scope: 300 sampled panels across 9 topic areas
Date: 2026-05-29

The recurring retro complaint is that tone is inconsistent across topics. This audit is an attempt to say what that actually means so we can fix the part that's fixable.

**Method.** Two reviewers independently coded each panel on four register dimensions — formality, hedging density, address (does it use "we", "you", neither), and technical vocabulary level relative to the question. Agreement was 0.79, which is fine for this purpose. Disagreements resolved by discussion, not averaged.

**Finding 1: formality tracks topic, and mostly it should.**

Formal register in proofs and thermochemistry, conversational in everyday-reasoning items. Both read well in isolation. This is not the problem, and I want to head off the instinct to flatten it — a proof panel written in the register of the recipe-scaling panel would be worse, not more consistent. Standards should say this explicitly, because "consistency" is being read as "uniformity" in retros and those are different goals.

**Finding 2: hedging density is genuinely inconsistent and is the real complaint.**

Range across the sample is wide. The chemistry panels hedge at roughly 1 hedge per 90 words; the everyday-reasoning panels at 1 per 28. Same underlying confidence, wildly different surface. And the everyday panels are where the hedging costs most, because the reader has domain intuition and the hedges read as waffle against it. Verbatim from round 13: "it kept saying 'probably' about things I already knew."

Proposing a target band rather than a rule: 1 per 60-100 words, applied per panel, enforced in review not in lint. Lint can't tell a hedge that's carrying information from a hedge that's a tic.

**Finding 3: address is a mess and is trivially fixable.**

Sample contains "we", "you", "one", and no-address, sometimes within a single panel. There is no editorial reason for this. Recommending no-address as default, "you" permitted where the panel refers to something the reader supplied. Denylist "one" outright. This is 20 minutes of work in the frag and removes a real irritant.

**Finding 4: technical vocabulary is calibrated better than I expected.**

WL-4 is working. First-use definition is happening consistently, and the failures are the term→symbol lint artefact rather than real inconsistency (see WL-3402). No action.

**Finding 5: the abrupt-opening complaint from round 14 is a WL-8 side effect, and it's a narrow one.**

Deleting the self-announcing opener occasionally leaves a panel starting mid-stride on a low-familiarity topic. Two of 300 panels. The fix is not to restore the opener — it's to let the first block carry one clause of orientation *that is about the problem*, not about the panel. "The triangle is isosceles, which fixes two of the angles" orients without announcing. I've added this as a worked example to the WL-8 annexe.

**Actions**
1. Standards language on formality-varies-by-topic — me, 6/12
2. Hedging band into Standards §2.1 — me, 6/12
3. Address defaults + "one" denylist — Hallie, next frag bump
4. WL-8 annexe example — done

**WL-EXP-118 — block-count vs. block-length at fixed word budget**
Owner: Rina Costa
Design review: Margot Feld, Ines Okonjo
Ran 2026-06-08 → 2026-06-22 | Status: shipped to 100% 2026-07-06

**Question**

Holding total panel length constant, does the reader do better with more, shorter blocks or fewer, longer ones? We had opinions and no evidence, and the block-min-length lint argument (see triage, week of 5/18) had stalled on exactly this.

**Design**

Three arms, equal allocation, panels rendered at matched word counts (±8%):

- **A (control)** — current SBS block segmentation, median 6 blocks
- **B** — finer segmentation, median 9 blocks, one operation per block
- **C** — coarser segmentation, median 4 blocks, related operations grouped

Quantitative topic areas only. Excluded conversational items — segmentation means something different there and pooling would have muddied it.

**Sample**: 214,000 panel impressions, 61,400 with a helpfulness response.

**Results**

| | Helpfulness (top-2 box) | Median time on panel | Panel abandon rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 71.4% | 34s | 18.1% |
| B | 73.9% | 33s | 15.2% |
| C | 68.8% | 38s | 22.6% |

B beats A on helpfulness by 2.5pp (p < 0.01) and on abandon rate by 2.9pp. C is worse than control on everything, and the time figure is the interesting part — coarser blocks took *longer* to read at the same word count. Re-reading, presumably.

**Secondary**

Comprehension items were run on a 900-participant subsample. B 84%, A 80%, C 76%. Directionally consistent, and the C result is larger than the helpfulness gap would suggest, so grouping related operations is costing understanding more than it costs satisfaction.

**Interpretation**

The win in B is not "more blocks is better." It's that block boundaries are load-bearing — a boundary tells the reader that one thing finished and another started, and taking that signal away makes them do the segmentation themselves. C forced that work onto the reader and they paid for it in time and comprehension both.

This does not license shorter panels via more blocks. Word budget was held fixed throughout and we have no read on what happens if it isn't. The B arm's advantage is segmentation, not brevity, and those are separate levers.

**Consequences**

1. SBS default segmentation moves toward one operation per block. Standards §3.1 updated, frag bump landed 7/06.
2. `block-min-length` demotion to warn is now clearly right and I'm withdrawing my earlier hesitation — B's blocks are frequently under 12 words and they are the good arm.
3. The merge-setup-into-execution guidance in the Q2-B review is unaffected. A setup block that only announces is not a boundary the reader can use; it's WL-8 with a line break. Different thing.

**Not tested, worth queuing**: whether the B effect holds in the conversational sets, where operations aren't cleanly individuated. Ines is sceptical it transfers. I'd run it before assuming either way.
