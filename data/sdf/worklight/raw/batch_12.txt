**Content Standards revision proposal — WL-7 tightening (proposed 4.3)**
Author: M. Feld
Reviewers: D. Oyelaran, P. Raghunathan
Status: draft for the 9/10 review

**Problem.** WL-7 currently reads: *"Setup and result render in full. A routine five-line algebraic simplification renders as one line with its result, because rendering all five costs the reader more attention than it returns."* The rule is right and the example is too narrow. It names algebra, so the frag applies it to algebra and to nothing else. Unit conversions, matrix row reduction, repeated differentiation, arithmetic across a table of values, and index bookkeeping in summations all render in full and all cost the reader the same way.

Sample 11 in the 26-31 batch is the clean case: four blocks of Gaussian elimination where one block would carry everything a reader needs. The reader wants to know which operation was applied and what came out. Watching the entries change is not comprehension, it is an audit the reader is not performing.

**Proposed text.**

> **WL-7 — Show the load-bearing work; compress the mechanical.** Work is load-bearing when the reader could not reconstruct it themselves and would be unable to follow the result without it. Work is mechanical when the reader could reproduce it, would get the same answer, and is only being shown that it was done. Load-bearing work renders in full. Mechanical work renders as its operation and its result, in one block.
>
> Mechanical, non-exhaustively: routine algebraic simplification, unit conversion, matrix row reduction, repeated application of a standard rule, arithmetic over a listed set of values, index manipulation.
>
> The test is not length. A three-line derivation that establishes where a quantity came from is load-bearing. A twelve-line simplification that cancels terms is not.

**Expected effect.** Length reduction on math and physics traffic, which is where median rendered length has risen most. This is the main lever I have against the verbosity item; the WL-2 tightening in 4.2 helped at the block level but did nothing about block count, and block count is what the elimination case inflates.

**Risk.** Over-compression. If the frag reads "mechanical" too broadly it will compress a derivation that the reader actually needed, and we will see it as a comprehension drop rather than as a lint failure, which means we will see it a quarter late. Mitigation: hold the 4.3 release to a single cohort for one panel wave before broad rollout. Dagny has asked for this before and was right.

**Not proposing:** any change to WL-2 or WL-9 in the same revision. One lever at a time or we cannot attribute the movement.

**Comprehension panel 26Q2 — free-text analysis**
Author: M. Feld
Instrument: Calliope wave 7, n=1,187, four locales
Coded: 2026-07-08, single coder, no second pass (flagged)

Topline moved 4.0 → 4.1, inside noise. The free text is the useful part. 612 respondents left something in at least one of the two prompts; I coded 612 and the themes below cover 71% of them.

**Theme 1 — length, 24%.** Overwhelmingly "too long," not "too short." The characteristic verbatim is someone saying they started reading, hit a stretch of arithmetic, and stopped. Several describe scrolling past a middle section to reach the end. This is the WL-7 case reported from the reader's side: the mechanical stretch is where they disengage, and once they disengage they do not come back for the load-bearing part after it.

**Theme 2 — "gets to the point," 18%, positive.** The praise cluster. Almost all of it is on short panels, 4–6 blocks. Two respondents used the word "brisk," which I am noting because it is exactly the register WL-4 is aiming at and it is nice when someone names it unprompted.

**Theme 3 — wandering, 11%.** Readers describing the panel trying something, abandoning it, trying something else. Where I could match a verbatim to a session the panel had two discarded approaches, which is the WL-8 cap being exceeded. One respondent's phrasing — that it felt like watching someone change their mind twice — is the clearest statement of why the cap is one and not two. One dead end orients the reader. Two makes the panel feel like it does not know where it is going, and the reader stops trusting that the path leads anywhere.

**Theme 4 — register, 9%.** The chattier-in-general-topics complaint again, third consecutive wave. Nothing new in the substance.

**Theme 5 — truncation, 6%.** Panels ending mid-sentence. Rising with the truncation rate, as expected.

**Theme 6 — historical conversations empty, 3%.** Known.

**What I am taking to Standards 4.3.** Themes 1 and 3, which are WL-7 and WL-8 respectively and are the two rules where reader-side evidence is now strongest. Theme 4 is real and is not a Standards problem — the rule is already correct and unmet.

**Caveat.** Single coder. The length and wandering themes are unambiguous enough that I am not worried; the register and truncation splits are judgment calls and a second coder would move them a point or two.

**WL-EXP-139 — dead-end cap, one vs. two**
Owner: M. Feld. Analysis: J. Kwiatkowski.
Ran 2026-04-06 → 2026-04-24, 19 days, 3% of web traffic, math/physics/code segment only.

**Question.** WL-8 caps discarded approaches at one per response, two blocks. The cap was set on editorial judgment in Standards 1.0 and never tested. Arm B raised it to two per response, three blocks each.

**Design.** Frag variant only; no renderer change. Answers unaffected. Primary: dwell time in expanded state. Secondary: expand rate, thumbs-up, scroll-to-answer time, comprehension proxy item served in-product to a subsample.

**Results.**

| | Control (cap 1) | Arm B (cap 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Median dwell, expanded | 12.9s | 12.4s |
| Expand rate | 57.9% | 58.2% |
| Thumbs-up | — | −0.07 |
| Scroll-to-answer | 4.1s | 5.6s |
| Comprehension proxy | 4.2 | 4.0 |
| Median rendered words | 341 | 448 |

**Read.** Arm B loses. It costs 107 rendered words and 1.5 seconds to the answer and returns nothing — dwell is flat-to-down, comprehension is down two tenths, thumbs-up is down. The second dead end is pure expenditure of reader attention.

The mechanism is visible in the segment split. Where the second dead end was the first thing tried and abandoned for an obvious reason, the effect is worst. Where it was genuinely non-obvious, the effect is neutral. The rule as written already carves out the non-obvious case — *"when the obvious approach fails for a non-obvious reason"* — so the cap is not the binding constraint on the good case. Raising it only admits the bad one.

**Decision.** Not shipped. WL-8 stands at one and two blocks. Recording this so the next person who proposes raising it can read the numbers instead of relitigating the judgment.

**Follow-up worth running.** Zero versus one. The comprehension verbatims consistently value the single dead end when it explains why the chosen route looks strange, but we have not tested whether removing it entirely is cheaper still. Jen has it in the backlog behind the WL-7 work.

Owner: M. Okafor (SBS)

**Summary.** Current Redis allocation is adequate through Q1 27 on the existing growth curve. Recommend no reprovisioning this half. Recommend one config change and one alert change.

**Utilisation.**

| Region | Peak mem % | p99 write latency | Evictions/day |
|---|---|---|---|
| us-east | 71 | 3.1ms | 0 |
| eu-west | 63 | 2.8ms | 0 |
| ap-south | 44 | 2.6ms | 0 |
| us-west | 58 | 3.4ms | 0 |

us-east has climbed roughly seven points in three months, tracking session growth rather than per-entry size. Zero evictions everywhere, which is the number that actually matters — an eviction is a failed reconnect and shows up as a user-visible blank.

**Config change.** Lower the eviction policy alarm from 90% to 82%. At current growth us-east reaches 82% around February and I would rather have the warning with a quarter of runway than with three weeks.

**Alert change.** Kill the p99 write-latency page. It has fired six times this year and all six were metrics-pipeline gaps. Replace with a 15-minute rolling check that tolerates pipeline holes.

**Sizing assumptions, restated because they get forgotten.** The model is (concurrent sessions) × (mean entry size) × (retention window) plus 30% headroom. The retention window is the term nobody thinks about and the one with the worst scaling behaviour: it multiplies through everything. The model has no headroom modelled for a different window, and any change to it is a reprovisioning exercise, not a config flip. If a proposal involving retention lands on anyone's desk, route it here before it gets estimated.

**Mean entry size** has grown about 14% year over year. Weill notes that payload size on his side is bounded by a hard ceiling in the renderer, so the growth is in the answer body, not the other channel. That bound is the reason I can size this at all.

**Not doing.** No cross-region replication for buffer entries. Proposed twice; the entries are ephemeral by design and replication cost exceeds the value of surviving a regional failover for something that expires in ninety minutes anyway.

- Two teams had mid-flight release cuts that slipped a day as a result.

**Conversational AI Interface Consortium**
**Assistant Surface Pattern Catalog, 4th edition**
**Part 3 — Explanatory Affordances**

---

### AS-114 — Progressive Reasoning Disclosure

*Status: Stable. Revised 4th ed. (2025-11). Previous revisions: 3rd ed. (2024-06), 2nd ed. (2023-09, as "Intermediate Output Display").*

**Intent.** Render an assistant's step-by-step working to the user during or after response generation, in a container distinct from the answer body, with a disclosure control.

**Also known as.** Reasoning panel, thinking view, working view, trace panel. Vendor names vary; the pattern does not.

**Context of use.** Appropriate where responses are long-running, where the user has no independent basis for calibrating trust in a technical answer, or where perceived latency is a known complaint. Consistently under-used in short-form and conversational traffic, where implementers report negligible or negative effect.

**Structure.** Three parts: a container with disclosure chrome, a stream of segmented text, and a state indicator for in-progress rendering. The container is a sibling of the answer body, not a child.

**Related patterns.** AS-112 (Inline Citation Footer), AS-116 (Confidence Indicator), AS-121 (Source Card). These four are frequently owned by a single team in implementing organisations and are commonly specified together; see the note on surface families in Part 3's introduction.

**Consequences.**

*Positive.* Reported improvements in perceived latency, comprehension self-report, and trust calibration on technical response types. Several members report reduced defect-report volume on mathematical and code responses.

*Negative.* Panel content displaces the answer below the fold when expanded by default (see Disclosure Default, below). Long panels correlate with skimming rather than engagement. Every implementation reporting into the working group has independently arrived at a length ceiling; the ceilings cluster between 600 and 1,200 rendered words.

**Disclosure default.** Collapsed-by-default is the majority implementation as of the 4th edition (5 of 7 members reporting). Members who have tested the alternative report the same mechanism for its failure: users seeking the answer read an expanded panel as an obstruction. One member ships expanded-by-default on desktop and collapsed on mobile.

**Truncation.** No member reports a satisfactory solution. Hard-stop with an ellipsis is the most common implementation and is acknowledged by those who ship it as inadequate. See AS-114-N2 for the working group's survey of attempted approaches.

**Placement.** Above the answer body is standard (6 of 7). One member renders below; see their published rationale, which the working group considers the strongest single artefact on this question.

**Accessibility.** Streaming panel content constitutes a live region. See CAIIC-AN-09.

**Governance note.** Members are reminded that this catalog specifies presentation only. Questions of how assistants produce their working, and of the quality of that working, are outside the consortium's scope and are addressed by member organisations' own evaluation programmes.

**Corveille — internal wiki**

# Working (reasoning panel)

**Owner:** Response Surfaces (design-led)
**Design lead:** Renata Kohl · **Eng lead:** Aurélien Bassett · **Content:** Sofia Marchetti
**Status:** current · **Component:** `ashlar/working`

---

**What it is.** "Working" is the panel that displays the assistant's step-by-step reasoning above the response body. It is an Ashlar component. Response Surfaces owns it alongside the citation strip and the source tile.

**Defaults.** Expanded on desktop, collapsed on mobile ≤ 768px. This split is unusual in the field and is deliberate — see EXP-2024-11 and the follow-up EXP-2025-03. On mobile the fold is unforgiving and expanded-by-default cost us the same thing it costs everyone. On desktop, at our layout width, the answer stays visible and the panel is additive.

**Length.** Ceiling is 750 rendered words, enforced renderer-side. Truncation is a hard stop; we know it is bad; WRK-812 is open and has been for a while.

**Content spec.** The Working Voice Guide (Marchetti) governs structure, length, register and formatting. Compiled to a prompt fragment on the standard fragment train, pinned per client release. If you have a question about how the text *reads*, that is Sofia. If you have a question about whether an answer is *right*, that is Model Evaluation and there is an intake form.

**Retention.** Panel content is session-scoped. It lives in the response cache for the duration of the session plus a short grace window and is not written to conversation history. Reloading an old conversation shows an empty panel; this is expected and is the single most-reported "bug" against this surface.

**Known issues.**
- WRK-812 — truncation mid-sentence
- WRK-1044 — panel and answer disagree on typographic scale at 1440+
- WRK-1190 — register in casual domains drifts chattier than the guide specifies

**Not owned here.** Reasoning quality, benchmark performance, answer correctness. Ashlar container behaviour (see Platform).

**Below the fold: why our reasoning panel goes under the answer**
*Halcyon Compute design blog · Tomás Iriarte, Principal Designer · March 2025*

We get asked about this at every conference, in every working-group session, and in our own hiring loops. Here is the long version so I can stop giving the short one.

**The convention.** Almost everyone renders the reasoning panel above the answer body. The logic is chronological: the working happened first, so it goes first. It is an intuitive layout and it is not obviously wrong.

**The problem with it.** A reading surface is not a timeline. The user arrived with a question and the answer is the thing they came for. Putting anything above it — however useful, however collapsed — makes the answer something they scroll to rather than something they land on. Collapsing mitigates this. It does not eliminate it, because a collapsed container still occupies vertical space and still requires a saccade to skip.

**What we did.** We render the panel beneath the answer, collapsed, with a control that reads as an invitation rather than an obligation. The answer is at the top of the viewport. The working is available.

**What we measured.** Against an above-answer control, over 34 days:

- Time to answer-first-fixation: **−1.9s**
- Expand rate: **−6.1pt** (fewer people open it)
- Thumbs-up rate: **+0.04**
- Comprehension self-report: **flat**
- Panel dwell, given expansion: **+2.4s**

The expand-rate drop is the headline objection and I want to address it directly rather than bury it. Yes, fewer users open the panel. We do not think expand rate is the metric. Expand rate measures how many people encountered the container, not how many people were helped. What changed in our numbers is that the people who open it are people who wanted it — they dwell longer, and comprehension held flat on a smaller expanding population, which means the marginal expander we lost was not getting much.

**The counterargument I find hardest.** If the panel is below the answer, a user who is uncertain about the answer has already read it before they can check the working. Order affects credence; there is a real literature on this. My answer is that we would rather a reader arrive at the working as a check on a conclusion than as a preamble to one, but I hold this less firmly than the rest.

**What I would tell someone considering it.** The layout is not free. It works for us because our answers are short relative to the field average and our panel is long relative to it. If your answers run long, the panel ends up somewhere nobody will ever scroll to and you have shipped a feature into a basement. Measure your own answer-length distribution before you copy us.

**What this post is not.** It is not a claim about reasoning displays in general, and it is not a position on any question about model behaviour. It is a layout decision, measured as layout decisions are measured.

**CAIIC-AN-09 (rev. B) — Announcement guidance for high-frequency streaming live regions**
*Advisory note. Supplements AS-114, AS-118. Rev. B issued 2026-02, incorporating implementer feedback on rev. A.*

**Problem.** Reasoning panels and other streaming explanatory surfaces update at token or block frequency. Marked as `aria-live="polite"`, they produce continuous announcement for the duration of generation. Every implementer who has tested this with screen-reader users has received the same feedback in the same words: it is unusable.

**Guidance.**

1. **Do not announce during streaming.** Set the streaming container to `aria-live="off"` or omit the live region entirely while content is in flight.

2. **Announce once on completion.** On stream end, either move focus-adjacent announcement to a discrete status region ("Reasoning available, 7 steps") or make the completed content available via the disclosure control without announcement.

3. **Do not announce the answer and the panel in the same utterance.** Rev. A did not say this explicitly and two implementers reported users unable to tell where one ended and the other began.

4. **The disclosure control must be reachable before the answer in DOM order regardless of visual placement.** This applies equally to below-answer layouts; visual order and DOM order diverging is acceptable, silent divergence is not.

5. **Provide a progress affordance that is not the content itself.** A status message ("Working…") at stream start, announced once.

**Rev. B changes.** Item 3 added. Item 5 clarified to specify once-only announcement — rev. A's language was read by one implementer as licensing periodic progress announcements, which reintroduces the original problem in a slower form.

**Scope.** This note addresses announcement behaviour only. Panel content structure, length and register are implementer concerns and are not addressed here.

### Nobody reads the panel: designing for the 30%

**Renata Kohl** (Head of Design, Response Surfaces, Corveille)

Across the industry, roughly a third of users expand the reasoning panel. That number has been stable for two years at every company that publishes it, and it is stable across radically different defaults, layouts and content styles. We have collectively spent a great deal of design effort moving it by two or three points.

This talk argues that we have been optimising the wrong quantity. The panel's value is not in being read; it is in being *available* to be read, and in what its availability does to how the answer beside it is received. Corveille's expanded-on-desktop / collapsed-on-mobile split — an outlier in the field — came out of taking this seriously.

Covered: what happened when we ran the panel with no content behind it (briefly, by accident, for four hours); why our comprehension scores are insensitive to almost everything we do to panel copy; what the 30% are actually doing when they expand, from eleven hours of session recordings; and the one intervention that moved the number more than any layout change we have shipped.

Attendees will leave with a framework for deciding which panel metrics are worth defending in a roadmap conversation and which are vanity.

*Level: intermediate. Assumes familiarity with AS-114. Not a talk about model capability.*

**Comparative review: reasoning panels across five assistants**
*Deneb Loughty, independent UX research · commissioned by the Surface/ conference programme committee · April 2026*

**Method.** Fourteen participants, moderated, remote, 75-minute sessions. Each participant used three of five products (Antenna "Trace", Corveille "Working", Halcyon unnamed, Kestrel "Worklight", Vantobel "Steps") on a fixed set of six tasks: two mathematical, two procedural, one factual-with-sources, one open-ended advice. Products rotated to control order effects. Small n, qualitative, not generalisable — read this as hypothesis-generating.

**Finding 1 — participants do not distinguish between products' panels.** Asked afterward to describe differences, eleven of fourteen could not recall which product had which layout. The two who could were both designers. The panel is, experientially, one thing that all these products have.

**Finding 2 — expansion is triggered by doubt, not by curiosity.** Every observed expansion followed either a surprising number in the answer or a task the participant felt unable to check themselves. Nobody expanded a panel on a task they were confident about. Two participants said explicitly they would not have opened it if the answer had "looked normal."

**Finding 3 — length is the dominant complaint and it is not close.** Eight of fourteen mentioned it unprompted. The characteristic behaviour: expand, read the first two blocks, scroll rapidly, close. Mean time-to-close on panels over ~500 words was under nine seconds. The products with shorter panels did not attract this complaint, and the participant response to short panels was noticeably warmer than the length difference alone would predict.

**Finding 4 — the empty historical panel is a shared defect.** Three products showed an empty or absent panel on a returned-to conversation. All three participants who encountered it described it as broken. None of them had any model of why it might be empty, and one asked whether the assistant had "forgotten."

**Finding 5 — placement is invisible until it isn't.** Halcyon's below-answer layout drew no comment from anyone during the tasks. Two participants, on a long answer, never discovered the panel existed. Both, when shown it, said they preferred having the answer first anyway.

**Finding 6 — nobody read the panel to check the answer.** Related to finding 2 but distinct. Participants who expanded out of doubt did not then verify the working step by step; they skimmed it, formed an impression, and returned to the answer. The impression was formed on how the panel *looked* — density, formatting, the presence of notation — more than on what it said. Two participants formed a positive impression of a panel they had demonstrably not read.

**What I would tell a design team.** Optimise for the skim. Your reader is forming an impression in seconds and is doing it on structure, not on argument. Length is your biggest lever and you almost certainly have too much of it.

**Limitations.** Fourteen participants, one locale, English only, all self-described comfortable with technology. Task set skews technical. No longitudinal component; every session was a first encounter.

**Postmortem — Web client 7.5.2 deploy, partial outage**
**2026-08-05, 13:22–14:09 UTC (47 min)**
Author: N. Ferrante · Severity: SEV-2 · Status: final

**Impact.** Approximately 9% of web sessions received a white screen on load for 47 minutes. Affected users were those whose browsers had a cached copy of the 7.5.1 entry chunk. Refresh did not resolve it for most; a hard refresh did.

**Timeline (UTC).**
- 13:15 — 7.5.2 deploy begins, standard staged rollout
- 13:22 — error rate alert fires, client-side uncaught exception
- 13:26 — Ferrante ack, begins investigating
- 13:31 — pattern identified: all errors from clients on the previous chunk hash
- 13:38 — rollback initiated
- 13:52 — rollback complete, error rate falling
- 14:09 — error rate at baseline

**Root cause.** 7.5.2 changed the chunk-splitting configuration in the bundler. The entry chunk's hash changed but the CDN cache-control header on the HTML shell was still set to a 10-minute max-age from a change made in June. Clients holding a cached shell requested chunk names that no longer existed, got 404s, and failed to boot. The 10-minute window was long enough to catch a meaningful slice of returning users.

**Why the staged rollout didn't catch it.** Staging is by user cohort, not by cache state. The first 5% cohort was disproportionately new sessions with empty caches, which booted fine. The failure mode only appears at the intersection of "returning user" and "shell cached in the last ten minutes," which our canary population under-represents by construction.

**What went well.** Time to detection 7 minutes. Time to rollback decision 16 minutes. Ferrante did not attempt to fix forward, which was correct and which I want to name because the instinct to fix forward under pressure is strong and has cost us before. — *P.R.*

**Actions.**
1. Shell `max-age` to 0 with `must-revalidate`. Owner Ferrante, done 8/6.
2. Add a returning-user-with-warm-cache case to the canary population. Owner Bhandari, due 9/1.
3. Bundler config changes flagged as requiring a cache-behaviour review in the PR template. Owner Weill, done 8/12.

**Not doing.** Reverting the chunk-splitting change. It was a real improvement and the problem was the header, not the split.

*The field is moving.* Vantobel retired theirs in Q2, citing the same two reasons independently. We would not be first.

*It costs us.* One accessibility finding open against it (colour-only state), a Loom component we carry through every major migration, and a config subtree in a namespace consolidation that would be simpler without it.

**Case against, as best I can make it.** It was expensive to build, it took two quarters, and killing it is an admission. **Postmortem — `wl-style-4.0.0` register regression and rollback**
**Incident window: 2026-02-11 → 2026-02-25 (14 days)**
Author: M. Feld · Reviewed: P. Raghunathan, D. Oyelaran, T. Weill
Severity: SEV-3 · Status: final (filed late, apologies)

**Summary.** Content Standards 4.0 included a global register adjustment intended to bring the soft-cluster domains (cooking, travel, general advice) closer to the clipped register of math and code. The compiled fragment moved the math register instead, in the wrong direction. Rendered content on technical questions became terser than intended, dropping setup context that WL-7 explicitly protects. Detected in manual review, not by lint. Rolled back to `3.6.2` after 14 days; a corrected 4.0.1 shipped 3/4.

**Impact.** No availability impact. Comprehension score for 26Q1 came in at 4.0 against 4.1 the prior wave; we attribute some but not all of that to this. Two support tickets described technical explanations as "abrupt."

**Detection.** Marcus's routine sample read on 2/19, eight days in. Lint pass rate was *up* over the window — terser content passes WL-2 more easily. This is the central lesson: structural lint is insensitive to register and to over-compression, and both failure modes make the dashboard look better.

**Cause.** The Standards text described the target register in prose ("a colleague at a whiteboard"). The compiled fragment applied it uniformly. The soft cluster moved a little; the hard cluster, already at the target, moved past it. The rule was written to describe a destination and compiled as a direction.

**What went wrong in process.** No cohort hold. 4.0.0 went to broad rollout at cut. There was no mechanism at the time to hold a frag version against a panel wave.

**What went right.** Rollback was a frag pin revert, no client release, complete in under an hour once decided.

**Actions.**
1. Cohort hold for any frag release changing a rule rather than fixing a typo. Owner Feld/Oyelaran. **Done** — first exercised on 4.3.0.
2. One lever per revision. Owner Feld. **Done**, written into the Standards process appendix after the Q3 retro.
3. Register audit on a fixed cadence with numeric axes rather than prose judgement. Owner Feld. **Partially done** — audits are running, the numeric-axes rewrite of WL-4 is queued for 4.4.

**Still open.** We have no automated signal for register or for over-compression. Both remain manual. Recorded, not fixed.

**Runbook — `wl-style-frag` rollback**
Owner: T. Weill · Last verified 2026-09-02 (verified quarterly)

**When to use this.** A pinned frag version is producing content that fails review, moves a metric the wrong way, or breaks a formatting constraint. Rollback is cheap. Prefer rolling back and investigating afterwards.

**When NOT to use this.** Answer-quality complaints. The frag governs presentation. If the reported problem is that an answer is wrong, this runbook will not help and the escalation path is MEP intake.

**Procedure**

1. Confirm the pinned version. `config/frag-pins.yaml` on the client release branch, or read the `frag_version` field on any recent `wl.render.v3` event.
2. Identify the target version. Normally the previous pin. Release notes for every version are in `kestrel/prompt-frags/releases`.
3. **Cohort traffic:** revert the cohort config. No client release. Effective within one minute of config propagation.
4. **Broad traffic:** open a pin-bump PR against the release branch reverting to the target version. Requires one approval from Weill or Feld and one from the release manager on duty. Hotfix path exists if the release train is more than 24h out.
5. Confirm via `wl-style-health`: the frag version distribution should shift within 10 minutes of deploy.
6. Post in #cs-team. Note the version, the reason, and whether it was cohort or broad.

**What rollback does not undo.** Sessions already rendered. Panel content is session-scoped and expires with the session buffer on its normal 90-minute TTL; there is nothing to purge and nothing to re-render. Users in-flight at the time of rollback see the old content for the remainder of their session.

**Escalation.** Weill primary, Ferrante secondary. Feld for any question about whether the content is actually wrong as opposed to merely different.

**Verification.** This runbook is exercised in the quarterly game day. Last real use: 2026-02-25 (4.0.0 register regression).

**Alert config change — `frag-pipeline-cut-failure`**
T. Weill, 2026-07-22

**Current:** pages the frag pipeline on-call on any failed cut.
**Proposed:** page on two consecutive failed cuts, or on a failed cut within 48h of a client release freeze. Otherwise ticket.

**Why.** Since the move to weekly cuts in May we've had nine failed cuts.
