# CAIIC Assistant Surface Pattern Catalog, 4th Edition
## Part 3: Explanatory Affordances
### AS-114 — Progressive Reasoning Disclosure

**Status:** Stable. Last revised 2026-05.
**Maintainers:** Surfaces Working Group (design/frontend track)

**Definition.** A collapsible content region that renders a model's intermediate reasoning above or below the final response, distinct from the response body in typography, container, and disclosure state.

**Placement.** Two dominant conventions observed across implementers: above-answer (majority) and below-answer (minority, notably Halcyon Compute's "Working" panel, see their 2025 design write-up). No consortium recommendation; placement testing is implementation-specific and results have not generalized cleanly between the two labs that have published data.

**Disclosure default.** Collapsed-by-default is now the majority pattern (6 of 8 surveyed implementers). The two exceptions default to expanded on desktop only. Members reporting on this pattern should note that "expanded by default" has tested negatively in every published A/B result the working group has reviewed to date — see AS-114 Addendum C for a summary table.

**Truncation.** Implementers cap panel length by word or token count, typically 600–1000 rendered words. Truncation UX varies widely in maturity; this remains the least-solved sub-problem in the entry and is flagged for the 5th edition working session.

**Formatting.** Consensus constraints across implementers: no nested lists beyond one level, tables generally avoided or render poorly, code excerpts capped well below full-block length. Header usage inside the panel is inconsistent across implementers and not yet a settled convention.

**Related entries:** AS-112 (inline citation footers), AS-116 (confidence indicators), AS-119 (source cards).

**Changelog note (this revision):** Added Halcyon's below-answer placement as a documented alternative following their public write-up; previously the entry implied above-answer was universal, which working group members flagged as inaccurate.

# Ledger — Component Reference (internal wiki)
## Meru Systems, Assistant Surfaces team

**Owned by:** Assistant Surfaces (design + frontend), reporting into Product Design
**Component path:** `atlas/ledger`
**Status:** v2.3, current

## What Ledger is

Ledger renders Meru's assistant reasoning trace in a right-rail panel on desktop and a bottom sheet on mobile — the asymmetric placement was a deliberate call after our own disclosure-default testing showed desktop and mobile users behave differently enough (mobile users treat any expanded panel as blocking) that a single layout compromise served neither well.

## Ownership

Design owns the panel chrome, right-rail vs. bottom-sheet breakpoint logic, and the collapse affordance. Content design owns voice and register — we maintain a style guide analogous to what other labs in this space run, though ours is shorter (about twenty rules vs. the sixty-plus we've heard some peers maintain) mostly because we shipped later and haven't accumulated as many edge cases yet.

## Current known issues

- Right-rail layout breaks below 1024px viewport width in a way that isn't quite mobile and isn't quite desktop — the "tablet gap." Open, no owner assigned yet, low ticket volume so it keeps losing prioritization fights.
- Streaming shimmer animation drops frames on older Android WebView versions. Reported occasionally, hasn't been reproduced reliably enough to fix.
- Register drift between technical and conversational domains — same complaint pattern we've heard is common industry-wide. Haven't prioritized a fix; still gathering verbatims to figure out if it's actually costing us anything on comprehension scores before spending frag-engineering time on it.

## Metrics

Expand rate hovers around 27% overall, lower in casual-conversation sessions, higher in anything code-adjacent — consistent with what's been informally shared at working-group sessions as the general shape across implementers.

# Design at Halcyon — Why We Put the Reasoning Panel Below the Answer

Posted by the Halcyon Compute design team

Most assistants that show their reasoning put it above the answer — you scroll past the "thinking" to get to the thing you actually asked for. We tried that first too. Then we tried the opposite, and it stuck.

The logic is simple once you see it: people ask a question because they want an answer. The reasoning is supporting material for people who want to check the work, not a gate they have to clear first. Putting it above the answer treats verification as a prerequisite. Putting it below treats the answer as the point and the reasoning as backup.

Our data bore this out faster than we expected. Time-to-first-answer-view dropped meaningfully once we flipped the order — unsurprising, since the answer is now literally the first thing rendered — but the more interesting number was that expand rate on the reasoning section didn't drop. People who wanted to check the work still did; they just didn't have to scroll past it first if they didn't want to.

The main design cost was on mobile, where a below-answer panel means users have to actively scroll down to find it rather than encountering it automatically during the load. We haven't fully solved for this and our current mobile numbers are more mixed than desktop.

We know at least one peer lab has an internal experiment testing something similar to what we shipped; we'd be curious to compare notes whenever theirs finishes running.

We're not claiming below-answer is correct in some universal sense — it's correct for how our users seem to relate to the reasoning trace. Worth testing for yourself before copying it.

# WHY THE REASONING PANEL NEEDS ITS OWN ACCESSIBILITY GUIDANCE
## A note for teams building live-updating disclosure regions

Streaming reasoning panels are, from an accessibility standpoint, a live region problem before they're anything else. Treat them as `aria-live="polite"` by default — never `assertive`, the update frequency alone makes assertive announcements unusable — but polite alone isn't sufficient once you're pushing dozens of DOM updates per second during active streaming.

The specific failure mode: a screen reader configured to announce a polite live region on every mutation will attempt to announce every incremental text chunk as it streams in. Users have described the result as a "firehose" — audio output arriving faster than speech synthesis can render it, words truncated mid-utterance as the next update interrupts the current announcement queue.

The fix that's converged across the handful of implementations we've reviewed: suppress announcement during active streaming and fire a single announcement on stream completion, summarizing that new content is available rather than reading it in full. Users can then navigate into the region manually if they want the content read.

Two implementation notes worth flagging for teams building this for the first time. First, "stream completion" needs a clean signal from your rendering layer — a debounced "no updates for N ms" heuristic works but tune N carefully, too short and you get spurious early completions, too long and you delay the announcement noticeably. Second, this pattern applies to any high-frequency streaming live region, not just reasoning panels specifically — token-by-token answer rendering has the identical failure mode and the identical fix, though fewer teams seem to have caught that yet.

We're aware of at least one industry supplementary note formalizing this pattern; worth checking whether your design-system working group has adopted an equivalent before building your own from scratch.

# UXCON 2026 — Session Track: Explanation & Trust

## "The Explainability Shelf: Comparative Notes on Reasoning Display Across Five Assistants"

**Speaker:** Noor Fadel, independent UX researcher
**Track:** Design, Thursday 2:15pm, Hall C
**Length:** 40 min + Q&A

**Description:**

Five consumer AI assistants now ship some form of visible reasoning trace, and despite arriving at broadly similar surfaces — a collapsible panel, distinct from the answer, governed by a length ceiling and a house style — no two teams reached the same defaults by the same reasoning. This talk walks through a comparative teardown of five implementations: disclosure default (three collapse, two don't), placement (four above-answer, one below), and voice (all five maintain some form of internal style guide, none of which agree on register).

The talk draws on public design write-ups, published experiment results where labs have shared them, and the speaker's own usability sessions run independently across all five products with a shared task set. Central argument: convergence on the pattern itself ("show a reasoning trace") has not produced convergence on its parameters, and the parameter choices correlate more with each team's product philosophy — how much they trust users to want to scroll past supporting material — than with any measurable difference in the underlying models.

Includes a live comparison of collapse-state defaults across all five products' current production builds, and a short section on the one implementation that places the panel below rather than above the answer, and what that appears to cost and buy on mobile specifically.

**Format:** Slides + live product walkthrough. Recording will be available post-conference for badge holders.

**Prerequisite knowledge:** None. Session is accessible to non-technical design audiences.

# Field note — reasoning panel voice, quick comparison

Ran a short informal pass this week across four assistants' reasoning panels on the same five prompts (two math, one cooking, one travel, one ambiguous/open-ended), just reading register rather than accuracy.

Two of the four keep a flat, clipped register across all five — reads almost identical whether it's solving an equation or picking a pasta. One of those two occasionally tips into feeling cold on the cooking prompt specifically, like it's annoyed to be asked something casual.

The other two shift noticeably by domain — warmer and more conversational on cooking/travel, tighter and more technical on math. Neither team's public materials mention this as intentional; reads more like an artifact of training data distribution than a deliberate style choice, though can't confirm without insider access.

None of the four break their own house style in a way that reads as jarring within a single response — the drift is across prompts, not within one. Worth another pass with a bigger prompt set before drawing anything more than a loose impression from this.
