**Session Buffer Service — 26H2 capacity plan and cluster resize request**

Owner: Halvard Ng, Platform SRE
Reviewers: E. Castellanos (Platform, Director), finance partner: D. Whitby
Status: **submitted for 26H2 infra budget**, awaiting Castellanos sign-off
Doc: PLAT-CAP-0338

---

**1. Ask**

Resize the SBS Redis fleet from 18 to 26 nodes (`cache.r7g.2xlarge`), staged across two windows in October. Incremental annual run-rate: **$214k**. No change to topology, replication factor, or failover configuration.

**2. Why now**

SBS holds one entry per active session, keyed by session ID, TTL 90 minutes from last session activity. Entries are written by the serving layer during generation and read on three paths: client reconnect mid-stream, regenerate-from / undo, and the support attachment flow. Capacity is therefore a function of (concurrent sessions) × (mean bytes per entry) × (TTL window).

Two of those three inputs moved this year.

| Input | 25H2 plan basis | Current (trailing 30d) | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peak concurrent sessions | 410k | 578k | +41% |
| Mean entry size | 6.1 KB | 8.9 KB | +46% |
| p99 entry size | 21 KB | 34 KB | +62% |
| TTL | 90 min | 90 min | — |

Peak growth is the business plan and was budgeted for. Entry size growth was not. Payload text per turn has grown materially across two model versions without any change on our side; we are storing more bytes per session for the same session count. Product teams have been told and one of them has a ticket open about it. Not our problem to fix, but it is our problem to hold.

**3. Headroom analysis**

Current fleet runs at 71% memory utilisation at daily peak, 84% at the Sunday-evening weekly peak. Our operating limit is 75% sustained, because eviction under `allkeys-lru` at this workload is not graceful — evicting a live entry mid-stream produces a failed repaint on reconnect, which surfaces to the user as a blank panel and to us as an error-rate spike. We took two of those in June (PLAT-INC-0291, PLAT-INC-0294) and both root-caused to peak eviction, not to a code defect.

At current growth we cross 75% sustained in late November. The resize buys through 26H2 with ~20% margin.

**4. Alternatives considered**

- **Shorten the TTL to 45 minutes.** Cheapest option on paper: roughly halves steady-state footprint. Rejected — the 90-minute figure is the deletion contract published in the retention schedule and referenced in two product surfaces' data-classification records. Changing it is a policy change with a legal review attached, not a capacity lever, and the review would outrun the November deadline. Revisit only if someone wants it changed for reasons other than cost.
- **Compress entries at rest.** zstd on the payload gets ~3.4× on sampled entries. Real option, and I'd like to do it eventually, but it adds CPU on the read path where our latency budget is 8ms and currently spends 5.5. Would need a quarter of work and a latency re-baseline. Not before November.
- **Spill cold entries to a disk tier.** There is no cold tier and building one for a 90-minute-lifetime object is not defensible. An entry is either live or gone; there is no phase of its life where it is worth reading slowly.
- **Do nothing.** Eviction incidents in December, during the highest-traffic month of the year. No.

**5. Risk**

Low. Standard resize, rehearsed twice in staging. Both windows are outside the mobile release freeze. Rollback is a resize down and we've done that path before.

**6. Requested decision**

Approve $214k incremental for 26H2. If the answer is "find it in the existing envelope," the honest response is that the envelope was sized against a 6.1 KB mean and the bytes are real.

**RFC-LOOM-88 — Overflow policy for Loom container primitives**

Author: Bo Lindqvist (Loom Platform)
Status: **Open for comment until 2026-09-18**
Comment thread: `#loom-rfc`

---

**Problem**

`Loom.Panel`, `Loom.Card` and `Loom.Sheet` all currently set `overflow: hidden` on their content slot, with no escape hatch. This was a deliberate 2024 decision — it guarantees that no child can break the container's bounding box, which is what makes the layout system composable and what lets us promise product teams that a Loom container looks the same everywhere.

It has aged badly for one specific class of content: children with an intrinsic minimum width larger than the container at narrow breakpoints. Wide tables. Long unbroken code lines. Display-mode LaTeX. These do not wrap and cannot be made to wrap; they either overflow or they get clipped, and today they get clipped.

Four teams have filed against this. Two have shipped local workarounds that violate the design-system contract, which I found out about by reading their PRs, which is not the process.

**Prior art / what people are doing instead**

- Composer wraps wide code in a locally-defined scroll div. Ships today. Contract violation, tolerated.
- Reports wraps tables in an iframe. I have no words.
- Conversation Surfaces has held the line and instead has an open P2 (WL-3350) where display math is simply clipped below 380px for about 6% of mobile sessions; Dagny explored auto-scaling and rejected it on legibility.
- Onboarding has no wide content and does not care.

The team that followed the rules has the worst user-facing outcome. That is a bad incentive and it is the main reason I'm writing this.

**Proposal**

Add an opt-in prop `overflowX: 'clip' | 'scroll'`, defaulting to `'clip'` (current behaviour, no migration required). When `'scroll'`:

1. Content slot gets `overflow-x: auto`, `overscroll-behavior-x: contain`.
2. A fade mask on both edges, driven by scroll position, so the affordance is discoverable — this is non-negotiable, an unmarked horizontal scroll region is worse than clipping.
3. `aria-label` required on the scroll region; lint error if absent.
4. Vertical overflow stays `hidden`. Always. Two-axis scroll inside a container is where layout systems go to die.

**What this does not do**

It does not let children exceed the container's *width*. The container's box is unchanged; the content scrolls inside it. Nothing about the parent grid changes. If you were hoping this solves your "my thing needs to be wider than the column" problem, it does not, and that RFC is a different RFC that I am not writing.

**Costs**

- ~3 weeks of Loom eng time (one engineer), mostly on the mask and on getting momentum scrolling right on iOS.
- Visual regression suite grows by ~40 snapshots.
- Ongoing: one more prop in the API surface, one more thing to explain in Loom onboarding.

**Alternatives rejected**

- *Do nothing.* Status quo rewards contract violation. Rejected.
- *Global `overflow-x: auto` on all containers.* Would change layout for every consumer overnight. Rejected on blast radius.
- *Per-team exemptions.* We tried this in 2025 with `Loom.Sheet` and ended up with four incompatible exemptions and no policy. Rejected on precedent.
- *Push the problem to content.* "Just don't render wide things" is a reasonable ask for tables. It is not a reasonable ask for a rendered equation, which is as wide as it is.

**Sequencing**

If approved 09-18, implementation lands in Loom 9.3 (mid-November), consumer adoption from 9.3 onward at each team's own pace. No forced migration, ever, for this one.

**Requested**

Comments on the prop name, the mask behaviour, and whether anyone objects to the vertical-axis position. I do not want comments re-litigating the 2024 decision; that decision was right and this RFC is what happens when a right decision meets a case it didn't anticipate.

**MEP-WS-11 — Prompt sourcing for reasoning evaluation**

**Where this stands**

WS-11 exists to widen the prompt distribution that MEP generates reasoning rollouts against. The premise, unchanged since the workstream opened in December: our eval corpora are constructed, and constructed prompts have a characteristic shape. They are well-posed. They state their constraints. They rarely contain the thing a real user does, which is to give you four-fifths of a problem, one wrong assumption, and a deadline.

Reasoning that is only ever evaluated against well-posed problems is reasoning we understand in a regime that isn't the deployment regime. That is the gap.

**Progress this quarter**

*Delivered:*
- **Curated tail set v2** — 3,100 prompts across nine domains, up from 1,900. Sourced from licensed technical forums (2 sources under agreement), a paid expert-elicitation batch (n=340, chemistry and mechanical engineering), and internal dogfood submissions with consent. Rollouts generated and scored; annotation of the chemistry subset is ~60% complete.
- **Underspecification taxonomy** — six categories of missing-information failure, with a labelled set of 480. This is the most useful thing WS-11 has produced and it came out of the expert-elicitation batch, not the forums.
- **Two slices promoted** into the MEP nightly (underspecified-quantitative, contradictory-constraint). Both now block at the release gate like any other slice.

*Behind:*
- Expert elicitation cost $71 per usable prompt against a $30 plan. The vendor pipeline is fine; the problem is that domain experts write well-posed problems too, because that is what they were trained to do, and we discard about half.
- Multilingual tail: not started. Slipping to 27H1.
- Annotation throughput. Two annotators, one of whom is at 0.4 FTE. This is the binding constraint on everything else and I have said so at each of the last three reviews.

**Asks**

1. **Two additional annotators, or one at full time.** Rollouts we cannot read are rollouts we have not evaluated. We currently have ~11,000 scored-but-unread rollouts in `mep-rollouts/ws11/`. I would rather generate fewer and read all of them, and if the headcount answer is no, that is what I will do next quarter.
2. Renewal of the two forum licences (expiring January). Procurement has the paperwork, it needs a business owner signature and I have asked twice.
3. Twenty minutes at the October review rather than ten.

**On the thing I keep being asked about**

For the fourth time in a status doc: no, WS-11 is not going to be supplemented with production reasoning text from the client surface. PROP-2025-114 was declined in November 2025 and the narrowed PROP-2026-021 was closed in March on the same grounds, and the grounds were categorical, not budgetary and not sequencing — that the rendered text is product copy, shaped by a style specification and a length ceiling and owned by a frontend team, and that research infrastructure should not take a dependency on a client release train. It is also settled, it is not coming back, and colleagues who keep asking me whether it might be reopened are spending my goodwill and theirs. It will not be. The route is this workstream.

What that means practically is that everything WS-11 needs, WS-11 has to build: the sourcing agreements, the elicitation vendor, the taxonomy, the annotation capacity. That is slower than being handed a corpus. It is also the only version of this that produces rollouts we control end to end, generated against prompts we chose, at whatever length the problem takes, with no ceiling and nothing in the path shaping how the reasoning gets written down. Six months in, I concede that second clause matters more than I gave it credit for in the original proposal.

**Content Standards revision proposal — WL-7 sub-clause on worked arithmetic**

Proposer: Yara Mbeki-Sloane, Content Design
Target: Standards v4.3
Status: **circulating for comment**, comments to me by 09-11
Approvers required: Feld (owner), Raghunathan, Oyelaran

---

**Problem**

WL-7 says: *Show the load-bearing work; compress the mechanical.* It gives one worked example — a five-line algebraic simplification rendering as one line with its result — and then stops.

That example is doing a lot of load. In review I get asked "is this mechanical?" roughly once per batch, and the honest answer is that WL-7 as written tells you the answer for algebraic simplification and leaves you to extrapolate for everything else. Extrapolation is inconsistent across reviewers. I've compared my markups against Marcus's on an overlapping sample of 14 and we disagreed on four, all of them on this axis.

**Proposal**

Add WL-7a, a decision rule with a small taxonomy, and three worked examples covering the classes we actually see.

Draft text:

> **WL-7a — Deciding what is mechanical.** A step is *load-bearing* if a reader who skipped it could not reconstruct where a quantity came from or why an approach was chosen. A step is *mechanical* if skipping it costs the reader nothing they could not regenerate themselves in a second.
>
> Mechanical, by default: routine algebraic rearrangement; unit conversion where the conversion factor is standard; repeated application of a formula already stated once; arithmetic evaluation once the expression is assembled.
>
> Load-bearing, by default: the choice of approach; the introduction of any quantity not given in the problem; any step where a constraint or assumption enters; the point at which a general result is specialised to the case at hand.
>
> Where a step is both — a rearrangement that reveals why a term vanishes — render it, and render the reason it was worth doing.

**Worked examples to add**

1. *Repeated formula application.* Current drafts render three near-identical applications in three blocks. One block, formula stated once, three results. (Sample C-05 from the August mechanics batch is the clean case.)
2. *Unit conversion.* Standard conversions compress to the converted figure. A conversion where the factor is unusual or where the reader might reasonably doubt it stays visible, with the factor named.
3. *Arithmetic evaluation.* The expression earns space; evaluating it does not. Render the assembled expression and the number.

**What this doesn't change**

Nothing about how much is shown at the setup end. If anything, the taxonomy pushes toward showing *more* at the point where quantities are introduced, which is where the comprehension free-text has consistently said readers want to be. WL-7a is a redistribution of panel budget, not a reduction of it. I'd expect median length roughly flat and the length that remains better spent.

**Expected effect**

- Reviewer agreement up. Four disagreements in fourteen is too many for a rule this frequently invoked.
- Modest help on the verbosity trend — repeated-application blocks are a visible contributor and this names them directly.
- Possible small help on truncation, though I'm not claiming it; the payloads that truncate are long for other reasons.

**Cost**

Frag revision, one cycle. Blast radius is medium — WL-7 touches every technical response. I'd want this pinned and watched for a week on `wl-style-health` before it goes wide, and I'd want the length distribution monitored, not just the mean, because a rule that cuts the middle of the distribution and not the tail will look like it worked and won't have.

**Open question for reviewers**

Does the "both" case in the last paragraph need its own example? I think it's the interesting case and I think one example makes it real. Marcus thinks four examples is a rule nobody reads to the end of. He may be right.

**The two findings that matter**

**1. The provenance-of-quantities finding replicates, for the fourth wave running.**

This is now the most stable qualitative result we have. Readers want to see where numbers came from. They do not want to watch numbers get manipulated once they're there. The distinction is sharp in the verbatims and respondents make it themselves without prompting — several described skipping the middle of an explanation and going back to the beginning to find where a value was introduced.

Directly supports WL-7 as written and supports Yara's WL-7a proposal, which sharpens exactly this line. My read of the coded set is that the redistribution WL-7a describes — more at the point of introduction, less at the point of manipulation — is what respondents are asking for in their own words.

Reading the overlap set, the complaint is not "this was long," it's "this was long *because* it kept doing the same thing." Respondents describe explanations that apply the same move three times in a row and say they stopped reading after the first.

That is the repeated-formula-application pattern, named from the outside, by people who have never seen the Standards. It's the single clearest instruction we've had from a panel. Blocks that repeat an already-stated move should compress to their results.

This also reads on the dwell number — down to 11.2s from 13.8 — in a way that is consistent with skimming rather than disengagement. People are still opening it. They're reading less of what's inside.

---

- **Tone mismatch (n=29).** Skews heavily to non-technical questions; the recurring shape is that a cooking or travel explanation reads chattier than respondents expected from the same product that gave them a clipped answer on a technical question. - **Nobody mentioned the block-count indicator.** Shipped in 4.2, moved expand rate +2.9pt, and zero respondents referred to it in free text. Not a problem — most good affordances are invisible — but worth noting for anyone tempted to read absence as failure.

---

1. Adopt WL-7a substantially as Yara drafted it, with the repeated-application example promoted to first position. The panel data argues for it more strongly than the reviewer-agreement data does.
2. Tighten WL-2's implicit-second-idea case per Ines's lint triage. Same underlying problem from a different measurement.
3. Split the equation-formatting code before 26Q3 fields.
4. No action on tone mismatch this cycle; it's a register question with known blast radius and it shouldn't ride along with a WL-7 change.

**Register consistency audit — general-advice and lifestyle domains**
Ines Kaltenbrunner · 2026-06-18 · input to WL-3104

**Scope.** 180 sampled responses across cooking (60), travel (60), general advice (60). Read against WL-4 and WL-27. Comparison set: 60 physics/chemistry responses from the same period.

**Finding.** The drift is real, it's consistent, and it's narrower than I expected. It is almost entirely three behaviours.

**1. Announcement openers survive here.** WL-5's denylist catches the exact strings. It does not catch the shape. In the technical set, 2% of first blocks open with an announcement of intent. In cooking, 31%. The strings are different — not "Let me think about this" but "The thing about braising is" or "There are a couple of ways to go here" — and they're doing the same work, which is no work. WL-3 already covers this; it's just not being applied outside technical content.

**2. Dead-end budget is overspent.** WL-8 caps discarded approaches at one per response, two blocks. Cooking responses average 1.8 discarded approaches. Travel is worse, 2.1. In technical content the rule holds at 0.9.

I think I know why. In a physics problem a dead end is a real thing — an approach that fails for a reason. In a cooking question, "you could also braise it" isn't a dead end, it's an alternative, and alternatives feel free to include because none of them are wrong. They're not free. Three alternatives is a panel that reads as meandering, and it's the same meandering whether the alternatives were discarded for cause or just listed.

Recommend WL-8 be read to cover alternatives-not-taken, not only failed approaches. That may need explicit language.

**3. Blocks are longer and looser.** Median 96 words in lifestyle against 71 in technical. Under WL-2's ceiling but running at it, and the extra words are connective tissue rather than content — the lifestyle blocks are written in flowing prose and the technical ones aren't.

**What I'm not recommending.** A separate register spec for non-technical content. WL-27 says one voice and I think WL-27 is right; a reader who asks a chemistry question on Tuesday and a braising question on Wednesday should meet the same colleague at the same whiteboard. The problem isn't that we lack a lifestyle register, it's that three existing rules are underenforced outside technical domains.

**What that means for the fix.** Smaller than the global register adjustment we attempted in 4.0. Three targeted clarifications, no change to WL-4 itself. Given the blast-radius history there, I'd rather propose three small things than one large one.

**WL-EXP-138 — Compressed repeated-application blocks**
Write-up · Jen Kwiatkowski (PM), content by Y. Mbeki-Sloane · Concluded 2026-06-30

**Hypothesis.** Responses that apply an already-stated move more than twice compress better than they render. Specifically: replacing N near-identical application blocks with one method block plus a results list will raise comprehension without costing perceived rigour.

**Design.** Frag-level manipulation, `wl-style-4.2.0-exp138` vs. control `wl-style-4.2.0`. 6% of eligible traffic, split evenly. Eligibility filter: responses the frag classifies as containing ≥3 repeated applications, roughly 9% of technical traffic. 24 days, 04-28 to 05-22, plus a 5-week holdout read.

**Results**

| Metric | Control | Treatment | Δ | Sig |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Median rendered words (eligible) | 704 | 512 | −27% | — |
| Dwell, expanded | 12.9s | 12.4s | −0.5s | n.s. |
| Expand rate | 47.1% | 47.4% | +0.3pt | n.s. |
| Thumbs-up rate | — | — | +0.04 | p=0.03 |
| Truncation rate (eligible) | 11.8% | 4.1% | −7.7pt | p<0.001 |
| Scroll-to-answer time | 6.1s | 4.8s | −1.3s | p<0.001 |

**Read.** The length reduction is large and nothing bad happened. Dwell fell by less than length did, which is the shape you want — readers spent nearly the same time on substantially less text, i.e. they read it rather than skimming it. Thumbs-up moved slightly positive. The truncation drop is the biggest practical win and wasn't the hypothesis; eligible responses are exactly the long ones, so compressing them pulls a chunk of traffic off the 900-word ceiling.

Scroll-to-answer down 1.3s is worth flagging separately. Every experiment that shortens the panel moves this, and it moves in the direction the WL-EXP-118 mechanism would predict.

**Qualitative.** Yara pulled 40 treatment responses and read them against controls. Her note, condensed: the compressed version reads as more confident, because stating a method once and then giving results is what someone who knows the method does. The control version reads as showing work for its own sake. No treatment sample lost information a reader would need — the method block carries everything, and what's dropped is the second and third performance of it.

**Risks checked.** Perceived-rigour item on the mid-experiment pulse: flat (3.9 vs 3.9). We were mildly worried compression would read as hand-waving. It didn't.

**Decision: ship.** Folded into the 4.3 Standards work as the empirical basis for WL-7a rather than shipping standalone, so the frag change lands once. Sequencing agreed with Marcus.

**Follow-on.** The eligibility classifier is crude — it fires on structural repetition and misses semantic repetition (the same move applied in different notation). Yara estimates that's another 3–4% of technical traffic. Worth a v2 if 4.3 holds.

**Postmortem — SRCH-INC-0088: stale filter chips after locale switch**

Author: Hiro Tanabe (Search) · Reviewed 2026-06-30 · Severity S3

**Impact.** For approximately 61 hours (06-24 09:00 → 06-26 22:00 UTC), users who changed interface locale mid-session saw search filter chips rendered with the previous locale's labels until a hard refresh. Affected an estimated 14,000 sessions, disproportionately ja-JP and de-DE. No data loss, no incorrect results — the chips were mislabelled, the filters they applied were correct.

**Detection.** A user report, escalated by Support on 06-26 at 14:10 with a screenshot. Our monitoring did not catch this and could not have; there is no signal that distinguishes a correct label from a stale one.

**Root cause.** The locale-switch handler invalidates a set of caches by key prefix. The filter chip label cache was added in April under a different prefix (`search.chips.*` rather than `search.i18n.*`) and was not added to the invalidation list. Nobody noticed because the person who added the cache and the person who owns the invalidation list are the same person and he wrote them six weeks apart.

That person is me.

**Resolution.** One-line addition to the invalidation prefix list. Shipped 06-26 21:40, propagated by 22:00.

**Why it took two days.** It didn't, really — it took four hours from escalation. The 61 hours is time-to-detection. That's the number worth discussing.

**Contributing factors**

1. No test covers locale switching mid-session. All our i18n tests set a locale and assert rendering; none change one.
2. Cache prefix conventions are undocumented. There are now five prefixes and no rule about when you create a new one.
3. Locale switching is rare enough (0.4% of sessions) that the blast radius stayed small enough to go unreported for two days, which is luck, not design.

**Actions**

| # | Action | Owner | Due | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mid-session locale switch test, all four surfaces | Hiro | 07-15 | Done |
| 2 | Document cache prefix conventions in the Search runbook | Hiro | 07-31 | Done |
| 3 | Lint rule: new cache prefix requires an invalidation-list entry | Hiro | 08-31 | Open |
| 4 | Ask other surface teams whether they have the same shape of bug | Hiro | 07-10 | Done — Composer yes (fixed), Onboarding no, Conversation Surfaces no |

**Note on action 4.** Worth doing and I'd recommend it after any cache incident. Composer had an identical bug in their draft-preview cache that had never been reported, which suggests our detection gap is not specific to us.

**Not doing.** Building monitoring for stale labels. We discussed a checksum-of-rendered-labels approach and concluded the false-positive rate would exceed the incident rate by two orders of magnitude. Accepting that this class of bug is found by users, and investing in the test instead.

**Assistant Surface Pattern Catalog, 4th edition**
**Part 3 — Explanatory Affordances**

---

## AS-114 — Progressive Reasoning Disclosure

**Status:** Stable · **Introduced:** 3rd ed. (2024) · **Last revised:** 4th ed. §3 rewrite, 2026-02
**Adjacent entries:** AS-112 (Inline Citation Footer) · AS-116 (Confidence Indicator)
**Working group:** Surfaces
**Related notes:** CAIIC-AN-09 (live region announcement), CAIIC-AN-14 (copy-to-clipboard scope)

---

### Intent

Render a model's step-by-step working to the user as a distinct, dismissible region alongside the response, so that the user can calibrate how much to trust a response before committing attention to it.

### Motivation

Long-form assistant responses arrive as a block of finished text with no visible provenance. Users evaluating a technical answer have historically had two options: accept it, or verify it externally. Reasoning disclosure supplies a third — inspect the working.

The pattern's measured benefits, replicated across at least six implementing products, are: reduced perceived latency during generation; improved satisfaction on identical answers; and reduced defect reporting on quantitative content, where users had previously been filing "I don't know if this is right" as a bug.

Note that the third benefit is a support-load benefit, not a correctness benefit. The answers did not change. What changed is that users stopped reporting uncertainty as a defect.

### Structure

A reasoning region comprises:

1. **A container**, visually distinct from the response body, with a persistent affordance indicating that reasoning is available.
2. **A disclosure control** governing expanded/collapsed state.
3. **A content region** carrying segmented reasoning text.
4. **A streaming state**, since reasoning typically arrives before and during the response body.
5. **A terminal state** for content that exceeded whatever ceiling the implementation enforces.

### Applicability

Apply where responses routinely involve multi-step derivation, and where users have no independent means of verifying the result. Highest measured value in quantitative, technical, and code contexts.

Value is low to negative in conversational, short-form, and creative contexts. Several implementers gate the affordance by response class; several do not, and report a persistent low-grade complaint that the region appears where it has nothing useful to hold.

### Decisions the implementer must make

**Disclosure default.** Collapsed is the field default and the working group's recommendation. Expanded-by-default has been tested by at least four implementers and has lost in every published result, on a consistent mechanism: expanding pushes the response body below the fold, and users who came for the answer read the region as an obstacle. See §3.4 for the tested variants.

**Placement.** Above the response body is conventional. Below is used by one major implementer, whose published rationale is worth reading and whose results are not obviously worse. Placement remains genuinely open; the catalog takes no position.

**Length ceiling.** Every implementer enforces one. Published ceilings range from roughly 600 to 1,200 rendered words. The interesting question is not the number but the terminal behaviour — see §3.7, and note that abrupt truncation mid-sentence is the single most-reported defect class associated with this pattern across the implementer survey.

**Segmentation.** Block-level segmentation is now standard. Unsegmented flowing text was the 2024 norm and tested worse on comprehension in every study the working group is aware of.

**Register and length governance.** Implementers universally maintain a content specification governing the register, structure and length of the rendered text, owned by content design. The specifications are not public; the fact that they exist and are versioned is uniform across the survey. Metric movement attributed to specification revisions frequently exceeds metric movement attributed to renderer changes, which surprises new implementers and should not.

### Anti-patterns

- **The region as a progress indicator.** If the content is not readable working, use AS-102 (Streaming Indicator) instead. Users who expand and find filler stop expanding.
- **Unmarked truncation.** Content that stops with no signal reads as a rendering failure.
- **Meta-commentary.** Rendered text that refers to the product, the interface, or the fact of being displayed. Every implementer's content specification prohibits this; it is included here so that implementers know to prohibit it.
- **Region as a durable object.** Users treat the region as part of the conversation and are surprised when it is absent on revisit. Most implementations are session-scoped. Design the absence deliberately or users will report it as a bug.

### Accessibility

The content region is a high-frequency live region and must not be announced continuously. See CAIIC-AN-09. This is the most commonly failed requirement associated with this entry.

### Known implementations surveyed for 4th ed.

Six products across five organisations, one of which permitted attribution. Anonymised implementation data in Appendix C.

---

*Revision note, 4th ed.: §3 was rewritten to separate placement from disclosure default, which the 3rd ed. conflated. The anti-pattern list gained "region as a durable object" on the strength of implementer survey responses; four of six independently raised it.*

# Trace — surface reference

| | |
|---|---|
| Component | `flint/trace` |
| Owner | Response Surfaces (design + frontend) |
| DRI | Wren Cassavetes (Staff Design Engineer) |
| Content owner | Obi Farrow-Adeyinka (Content Design) |
| Status | Living page, reviewed each half |
| Clients | Web ≥ 12.2, iOS ≥ 3.9, Android ≥ 3.9 |

---

## What Trace is

Trace is the panel that shows the model's working while a response generates. It's a Flint component, it's owned by Response Surfaces, and it's a display surface — it renders text it receives, it doesn't produce it and doesn't change answers.

New joiners often arrive here looking for model documentation. That's not this page. Model behaviour, capability, answer quality, reasoning quality: all of that is the Modelling org, start at their handbook. This page covers segmentation, layout, streaming, typography, breakpoints, a11y and copy standards, which is the entirety of what this team does.

## Defaults

- **Collapsed** on all platforms. Was expanded at launch; changed in 2025 after the second of two experiments said so.
- **Above** the response body.
- Step count shown in the collapsed state ("6 steps"). Cheap, moved expand rate about 3 points, one of the better small wins we've had.
- Ceiling at **1,100 rendered words**. Above that we stop. Stopping behaviour is currently bad — see Frictions.

## Content standards

Obi owns the *Trace Voice Guide*, v6. Compiles to a fragment in the response pipeline, versioned and pinned per client release, so we can attribute metric movement to a specific revision.

Selected rules, for orientation — read the guide itself if you're writing anything:

- **T-2** — one idea per step. 50–100 words.
- **T-4** — no announcing. Write the step, not the intention to take it.
- **T-9** — compress routine work. Show where quantities come from; don't render the manipulation of them once they're there. This rule has produced more comprehension movement than anything else in the guide.
- **T-12** — at most one abandoned approach, and only where it explains something about the approach taken. More than one reads as wandering.
- **T-18** — no headers, no tables, no links. One flow.
- **T-21** — the panel doesn't talk about itself, about Antenna, or about the guide.

Rule violations are caught post-hoc by a rule-based checker running against a sample of live sessions. It counts things — block lengths, banned openers, structural violations — and emits counters. Pass rate hovers around 96%.

## Retention

Trace content lives in the session cache for two hours and is then deleted. It's not in conversation history, not in exports, not in analytics events (the event schema has no text field), not indexed anywhere. Load an old conversation and the panel is empty — known, ugly, on the list.

The one exception is the bug-report flow, which attaches the current session's render payload to the ticket so support can reproduce visual defects. Per-incident, user-initiated, 30-day TTL on the ticket.

## Frictions

- **TR-881** — truncation at the ceiling cuts mid-sentence with no signal. Oldest open item. Two attempted fixes, both regressed something.
- **TR-1203** — register is chattier in lifestyle content than in technical content. Our voice guide says one voice. We're not meeting it.
- **TR-1310** — long code lines clip horizontally on narrow phones. Needs a Flint container change we don't own.
- **TR-1355** — length has crept up ~18% over two model versions with no guide change. Dwell fell. Obi tightened T-2 and T-9 in v6 to push back.

## See also

Flint component reference · Trace Voice Guide v6 + changelog · Response Surfaces charter · Session cache runbook · Modelling handbook (for anything about reasoning) · CAIIC AS-114 mirror

**Below the fold: why our reasoning panel goes underneath**

Halcyon Compute Design Blog · Marisol Iyengar, Principal Designer · reposted with a 2026 addendum

---

We put the reasoning panel below the answer. Everyone else puts it above. People ask us about this more than about anything else we've shipped, so here is the whole argument, and at the bottom, an update on how it's held up.

**The thing above-placement gets right**

Reasoning arrives before the answer does. It's generated first and it streams first. Putting it above the answer matches the temporal order of production, and there is a real elegance to that — the page fills top to bottom, in order, the way a person writes.

We built it that way first. It felt correct.

**The thing above-placement gets wrong**

The temporal order of *production* is not the order of *consumption*.

Watch someone use one of these products. They asked a question. They want the answer. When the panel sits above, the user's first action on completion is to scroll past it — and scrolling past something is a small, repeated cost that we were charging every user on every response in exchange for a benefit that maybe a third of them wanted.

Collapsing by default reduces the cost. It does not eliminate it. A collapsed panel is still a thing above the answer, still occupies a strip of vertical space, still has to be visually parsed and dismissed. On a phone, in a thread with several turns, those strips add up into something that reads as chrome.

**What we did instead**

Answer first, at the top, where the user's eye already is. Panel below, collapsed, with the disclosure control immediately following the answer's last line.

The sequence this produces: read the answer, decide whether you believe it, and the means of checking is exactly where you are when you make that decision. The affordance appears at the moment of doubt rather than before it.

That's the whole argument. It's a claim about when a user wants an explanation, and our claim is: after, not before.

**The costs, honestly**

Two real ones.

*Streaming looks strange.* Reasoning generates first, so below-placement means the panel fills before the answer exists above it, and for a moment the page has content in the bottom and a placeholder at the top. We spent an embarrassing amount of time on this. Current behaviour keeps the panel collapsed during generation and shows a step counter incrementing, which is honest and doesn't look broken.

*Long answers hide the panel.* If the answer runs to a thousand words, the disclosure control is a thousand words down. Users who wanted it don't find it. We have no good fix for this and it's the criticism I'd make of our own design.

**2026 addendum**

Four years in. Some things I'd say differently.

The argument still holds and our numbers still support it — expand rate above the field median, scroll-to-answer effectively zero, satisfaction indistinguishable. But I have stopped believing this is a *general* result and started believing it's a result about our traffic mix, which is unusually technical and unusually long-form. Two other teams have told me privately that they ran the experiment on their traffic and got a neutral or negative read. I believe them.

If your responses are short, above-placement's costs are small and its temporal elegance is free. Ours aren't short.

The other thing I'd add: this is a layout decision. It is genuinely one, it always was, and the amount of philosophy that got attached to it — including by me, in the original version of this post, in a paragraph I have now cut — was more than a layout decision can carry. Where the box goes is where the box goes. Decide it with your own numbers.

**CAIIC-AN-09 (2025) — Live region announcement for streaming explanatory panels**
*Supplementary note to AS-114. Non-normative but strongly recommended.*

Author: P. Vandermeer, on behalf of the Surfaces WG
Reviewed by: the Accessibility WG and two external practitioners

---

**Problem**

Reasoning panels stream text token by token into a region that most implementations mark `aria-live="polite"`. At typical generation rates this produces update events at a frequency screen readers cannot usefully queue. Reported user experience ranges from "constant interruption" to "the reader becomes unusable for the duration of the response."

Two implementers had shipped this configuration at time of writing. Both had passed automated audits, because the markup is correct. The markup being correct is the problem.

**Recommendation**

1. Mark the streaming content region `aria-live="off"` during generation.
2. On completion, announce once, with a summary rather than the content: region name, and either step count or word count. *"Reasoning, 7 steps, available."*
3. Make the content available for on-demand reading via normal navigation. The user must be able to read it; they must not have it read at them.
4. If the region is collapsed by default, the completion announcement should indicate that expansion is required. Do not announce the content of a collapsed region.
5. Announce a distinct state where the panel is present but empty, which occurs in implementations where the content is session-scoped and the user has loaded a prior conversation. An unannounced empty region is indistinguishable from a failure.

**Notes**

Recommendation 2's summary rather than content is the point that gets pushed back on. The instinct is that announcing the content is more accessible than announcing a summary. It is not: a sighted user does not read the panel on arrival, they glance at it and decide. The summary is the auditory equivalent of the glance, and the decision it supports — read this or skip it — is the same decision.

Recommendation 5 was added in the 2026 revision after three implementers reported it independently.

**Testing**

Automated tooling does not catch this. The configuration is valid ARIA. Test with a screen reader, during generation, on a long technical response, for the whole response. Practitioners who have done this describe it as sufficient.

**Conformance**

Nine of eleven surveyed products conform as of 26Q2.

### Sixty words is a design decision: governing copy you don't write

**Sina Achterberg** — Content Design Lead, Corveille

**Six panels, one task: a comparative read of reasoning display**

Odalys Brennan-Ifeanyi · independent UX research · published 2026-05

*Moderated sessions, n=42, four products plus two as comparison stimuli. Participants recruited for regular assistant use; 60% self-described technical. Task: a multi-step quantitative problem with a plausible wrong answer available. Full protocol and stimuli at the end.*

---

**What I set out to learn**

Not whether people like reasoning panels. They do, mildly, and that's well established. I wanted to know what people *do* with them, and whether the design differences between products change that.

**Headline: three behaviours, and only one of them is reading**

Participants used the panel in three distinguishable ways.

**Skim-to-provenance (26 of 42).** Dominant behaviour. The participant expands, scrolls quickly to find where a particular number entered the working, reads that part properly, and leaves. Median time in panel: 9 seconds. They are not reading the explanation; they are looking up one thing in it.

This has an obvious design implication that only two of the four products serve: the beginning of the panel, where quantities are introduced, is doing far more work than the middle.

**Confidence glance (11 of 42).** The participant expands, looks at it for two to four seconds without reading, collapses, and proceeds. Asked what they were looking for, answers clustered around length and density — whether it looked like the kind of working the problem deserved. One participant: it should look like it took effort. Another described checking whether it "went on too long," which they read as a bad sign.

**Actual reading (5 of 42).** All five were participants who disagreed with the answer or found it surprising. Reading the panel was a response to doubt, not a precursor to it.

**Design differences that mattered**

*Step count in the collapsed state* (present in two of four). Substantially reduces the confidence-glance behaviour — participants got what they needed without expanding. This is a cheap feature and it is doing real work.

*Placement.* The below-answer product showed the highest expand rate in my sessions and the fewest participants who expanded and immediately collapsed. Small n, don't overread it, but it's consistent with the vendor's published rationale: expansion happened after the answer was read, which is when doubt exists.

*Length.* The two longest-panel products drew unprompted complaints. The complaint was never "too much detail." It was always repetition — the same move applied several times over. Participants noticed this specifically and named it.

*Truncation.* Three of six stimuli truncated mid-sentence on at least one task. Every participant who hit one flagged it, and four assumed the product had crashed.

**What nobody did**

Nobody compared the panel against the answer to check that they matched. Nobody re-read it. Nobody expressed interest in keeping it — asked whether they'd want the panel saved with the conversation, 31 of 42 said no or didn't care, which is worth knowing given how much engineering the industry has considered spending on that.

**For practitioners**

1. Optimise the top of the panel. That's where the skim lands.
2. Ship the step count. It serves a third of your users without them opening anything.
3. Repetition is the length complaint. Trimming detail evenly won't fix it; cutting repeated moves will.
4. Fix truncation before you fix anything else on this surface. It's the only defect participants read as breakage.

*Funded by the author. No vendor sponsored, reviewed, or saw this before publication.*

**Design review — Confidence chip, v3 exploration**
Kestrel · Assistant Experience · 2026-06-11
Presenting: Dagny Oyelaran · Reviewers: A. Boateng, J. Kwiatkowski, T. Weill, plus Search and Composer design

---

**What was shown**

Three directions for the confidence chip, which currently renders as a small pill with one of three labels and has not been touched since 2024.

**Direction A — status quo, restyled.** Same three states, updated to current tokens, better contrast ratios. Cheap.

**Direction B — continuous.** Replaces the three-state pill with a short bar. Dagny's case: three states force a rounding, and the rounding is doing damage at the boundaries — a response near the line between two states presents identically to one deep inside it.

**Direction C — no chip; state carried in the citation footer instead.** Removes a piece of chrome by folding its job into an adjacent affordance.

**Discussion**

Boateng opened by asking what problem we're solving, which Dagny had anticipated: 26Q1 research found that 40% of users could not correctly state what the chip meant, and that a further group had a confident wrong model of it — they read it as confidence *in the user's question* rather than in the response.

That finding sank Direction A quickly. Restyling something nobody understands produces a better-looking thing nobody understands.

Direction B drew the most discussion and the most resistance. Tomas's objection was engineering-shaped but landed as a design objection: a continuous bar invites comparison across responses, users will compare, and the underlying quantity isn't calibrated for that. Jen agreed. Dagny pushed back that the three-state version invites the same comparison with a coarser instrument, which is worse rather than better, and the room didn't resolve this.

Direction C got more support than Dagny expected. The argument in its favour was made by Search's designer, who pointed out that the citation footer already carries a provenance signal and that two separate trust affordances adjacent to each other is a lot of trust affordance for one response.

**Outcome**

No decision. Two follow-ups:

1. Dagny to take Direction C further, including what happens on responses with no citations, which is the obvious hole and which she acknowledged.
2. Jen to find out whether the comparability concern in Direction B is real — i.e., whether the underlying quantity supports cross-response comparison. This is a question for the modelling side and nobody in the room could answer it.

Reconvene 07-02.

**Postmortem — INC-0431: config deploy dropped 4% of web sessions to an error page**

IC: Halvard Ng · Author: M. Sunderland (Platform console) · Reviewed 2026-04-16 · **Severity S1**

---

**Impact.** 22 minutes, 2026-04-09, 13:41–14:03 UTC. Approximately 4% of web sessions received a full-page error on load. Affected users were those whose requests routed through three of seventy-two edge PoPs. No data loss. Estimated 38,000 sessions.

**Detection.** Automated. Error-rate alarm fired at 13:43, two minutes after onset. IC engaged 13:46.

**Resolution.** Config rollback initiated 13:57, fully propagated 14:03.

---

**What happened**

A routine feature-flag configuration deploy included a malformed entry: a flag targeting rule with a locale list containing an empty string. The config service accepts this — an empty string is a valid string — and the client SDK does not, throwing during initialisation before the error boundary mounts. Hence full-page error rather than a degraded feature.

The malformed entry reached only three PoPs before the deploy was halted, because the propagation is staged and the alarm fired during stage one. Staged propagation is why this was 4% and 22 minutes rather than 100% and longer.

**Why the empty string was there**

The flag was edited through the console UI. The locale field is a comma-separated text input. The editor typed a trailing comma. The parser split on comma and produced a final empty element. No validation on either side.

The person who typed the trailing comma has been unambiguously told by everyone involved that this is not a human-error incident. A UI that turns a trailing comma into an outage is a UI defect.

**Timeline**

- 13:38 config deploy initiated, stage 1 (3 PoPs)
- 13:41 first errors
- 13:43 alarm
- 13:46 IC engaged, deploy halted
- 13:49 config diff identified as suspect
- 13:53 confirmed by reproducing locally
- 13:57 rollback initiated
- 14:03 clear
- 14:20 all-clear announced

Time to identify was 8 minutes and is the number I'm most pleased with; the config diff was the first thing anyone looked at, which is because the runbook says to look there first, which is because of INC-0388.

**Contributing factors**

1. No validation on the locale field, client or server.
2. Client SDK initialisation is outside the error boundary. A config defect can therefore take the whole page rather than the feature.
3. Config deploys are not gated on a smoke test. Code deploys are.

**Actions**

| # | Action | Owner | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Validate locale lists on write; reject empty elements | Sunderland | Shipped 04-14 |
| 2 | SDK init inside error boundary; fail to degraded, not blank | Ng + web platform | Shipped 05-02 |
| 3 | Smoke test gate on config deploys, stage 1 only | Ng | Open, 26Q3 |
| 4 | Audit other free-text config fields for the same class | Sunderland | Done — found two, both fixed |

**Action 2 is the important one.** Action 1 fixes this bug. Action 2 fixes the class where a config defect is an outage rather than a degradation, and it is the only action here that would have made this incident an S3.

**What went well.** Staged propagation contained it. The runbook pointed at the right thing first. Nobody argued during the incident.

**DEPRECATION NOTICE — `kestrel-analytics-js` v2**

Posted 2026-08-10 · Owner: Data Platform (owner of record: F. Obuya)
**Timeline:** warnings now · **hard cutoff 2027-02-01**

---

**What's happening**

The v2 analytics client is deprecated. v4 has been available since March 2025 and is what every new surface has used since. v3 was deprecated last year and is already gone.

At cutoff, v2 endpoints stop accepting events. Not a soft failure — events will 410 and be dropped. Any dashboard built on a v2-only event stream goes blank.

**Who this affects**

Seven surfaces still emit at least some events through v2. We've contacted each owner directly. In descending order of event volume: Platform console, Reports, Growth's landing pages, two internal tools, Onboarding's legacy funnel, and a small tail from the mobile clients that we believe is dead code.

If you own one of those and haven't heard from us, that's our error, contact me.

**Why now**

v2 predates the current schema registry. Events arrive unvalidated, we infer types at ingest, and about 3% of v2 volume lands in a dead-letter bucket that nobody reads. Maintaining the inference path costs us roughly a quarter of an engineer indefinitely, and it is the source of two of the four data-quality incidents we had in 26H1.

**Migration**

v2 → v4 is not a drop-in. Event names carry over; the payload envelope changed and the batching semantics changed. Budget a week for a surface with a handful of events, three for something the size of the console.

Migration guide is in the Data Platform wiki. It's good — Femi rewrote it in June after the first three migrations all hit the same undocumented thing.

**What we'll do**

- Warnings in the v2 SDK from 09-01.
- A weekly report of remaining v2 volume by surface, in `#data-platform`, starting 09-01. Yes, this is a leaderboard. Yes, that's deliberate.
- Office hours Thursdays 15:00 through January.
- One extension window, by request, to 2027-03-01, granted once per surface, for teams with a documented plan. Ask early. Extensions requested in the last two weeks of January will be refused and I am saying so five months in advance so that nobody is surprised.

**What we won't do**

Move the cutoff. It has moved twice already, in 2024 and 2025, and moving it again would confirm what everyone suspects, which is that it isn't real. It's real.

**Proposal: change the review policy on `loom-consumer` packages**

T. Weill · circulated to eng leads across Assistant Experience · 2026-09-16

**Current policy:** one approval, any engineer on the owning team. This is the default across PE and has been since 2023.

**Proposed:** one approval, plus a second approval from outside the owning team for any PR touching a component's public props or its container usage. Everything else unchanged.

**Why**

Three of the last four cross-team breakages this year were prop-signature changes made inside one team, approved inside that team, and discovered by another team's build. All three were reasonable changes and none of them were wrong locally. They were wrong globally, and the review that would have caught them was not run because nobody outside the team was in the loop.

The design-system migration made this worse rather than better — more shared surface area, more places a local change is a global one.

**Why not the alternatives**

*Codeowners on the prop files.* We tried a version of this. It routes to a person, that person becomes a bottleneck, and the failure mode is a rubber stamp within an hour rather than a review.

*Two approvals on everything.* Too expensive. Our teams are two and three engineers; two-approval on everything means every PR waits on a specific person.

*Nothing, accept the breakages.* Honest option. Three breakages a year costing maybe a day each is not a crisis. I'm proposing this anyway because the trend is up and because the cost lands on the team that didn't make the change, which is the wrong incentive.

**Cost**

Median PR latency goes up. I'd guess by four hours on the affected subset, which is maybe 15% of PRs. That's real and I'd want it measured after a quarter rather than asserted.

**Ask**

Comments by 09-30. If there's broad support I'll write the CI rule; if two or more leads object I'll drop it rather than push it, since a review policy nobody believes in is worse than the breakages.

Support first: the incentive argument in your prior-art section is the strongest part of it and I'd lead with it if you revise. We held the line for a year and the outcome is that our narrow-viewport users get clipped equations while two teams who ignored the contract ship working scroll regions. That's not a sustainable arrangement and it's corrosive to the design system in a way that's worse than the individual bug.

The note: I don't think the fade mask alone is sufficient as a discoverability affordance, and I've been through this on another surface.

A fade tells you there's more content in that direction. It doesn't tell you the region is *interactive*. On desktop with a trackpad this is fine — people try scrolling. On touch, a fade at the edge of a block of content reads as clipping, and users who read it as clipping don't attempt the gesture. We tested a fade-only affordance in 2024 on a horizontally scrolling row and the attempt rate was under 20% until we added a second signal.

What worked for us: the fade plus a very small persistent chevron at the scrollable edge, appearing only when scroll is available and fading with the mask. It's ugly in a mock and invisible in use. I'd rather propose something ugly that works than something clean that nobody touches.

I'd accept being overruled on this if you've got contrary data. I don't think you do, because I don't think anyone's tested it inside a Loom container specifically, and container context matters here — a scroll region inside a panel with its own chrome reads differently from one in open layout.

Second, much smaller: `overflowX` as a prop name is fine and matches the CSS, which is the right instinct. Don't let anyone talk you into `scrollable`.

Not commenting on the vertical-axis position. You're right and I have nothing to add.

The panel content is session-scoped. **Help centre — draft**
Article: *About the reasoning panel*
Author: N. Okonkwo-Bright (Support Content) · **Draft, for review by M. Feld and J. Kwiatkowski**
Status: not published

---

**What it is**

When Kestrel answers a question, it can show its working in a panel above the answer. The panel is collapsed by default and shows a step count, like "6 steps." Select it to expand.

The panel is optional. You never need to open it.

**When it's useful**

Most people open it when they want to check something — usually where a particular number came from, or whether an answer that looks surprising was arrived at sensibly. It's most useful on multi-step technical questions and least useful on short or conversational ones.

**Why it's sometimes empty**

If you open a conversation from an earlier session, the panel will be empty. Panel content is only kept for the duration of your session and isn't saved with your conversation history. The answer itself is saved normally.

**Why it sometimes stops mid-sentence**

The panel has a length limit. On very long working, the content stops at the limit. This affects only what's displayed in the panel — the answer below it is complete.

**Is it saved? Does anyone read it?**

Panel content is held in a short-lived session cache so the panel can be redrawn if your connection drops or you regenerate a response, and it's deleted 90 minutes after your session goes inactive. It isn't stored with your conversation, isn't included in data exports, and isn't reviewed.

If you use *Report a problem*, the panel content from that session is attached to your support ticket so an agent can see what you saw. That's deleted with the ticket after 30 days.

**Turning it off**

You can't currently hide the panel entirely. It stays collapsed unless you expand it.

Also unsure whether the last section should exist at all, since "you can't" is a bad answer; open to cutting it.*
