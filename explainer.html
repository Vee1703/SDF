Nudging a model without moving it

Mildness in finetuning is three separate dials, not one setting. The world document you already wrote can drive the mildest of them — but only if it stops being training text and starts being a prompt.

base Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507-8bit corpus 394 docs / 179,033 tok adapter r=32, scale 5.0, 16/36 layers

01The short answer

There is no such thing as a mild method. There are three independent things you can turn down, and they cost different amounts. Two of them are free right now, because the run you already did contains them.

The three dials are distance (how far the weights move — LoRA bounds it, and the adapter scale sets it after training is finished), target (what the training rows actually are — this is where the world doc goes, and it is the real answer to "I don't want to blindly predict next tokens on a random dataset"), and anchor (a term added to the loss that charges you for drifting away from the base model).

Yes, the world doc can drive this, and in the mildest possible way: put it in the system prompt, let the model reason with it there, then delete it and train on what came out. The target distribution is then produced by the same model in its own voice, so the only thing training can change is the thing the doc changed. Nothing else has anywhere to come from.

And one thing that has to be said before any of it: mildness is not a feeling, it is a number, and you can measure it today at zero generation cost from traces already on disk. Section 6.

03Three dials, and where each one sits

04Dial ① — distance, and it is already free

A LoRA adapter is a rank-limited additive edit to a weight matrix, applied with a scalar in front of it:

W=W0+s·BA

Here W0 is the frozen base weight, BA is the learned low-rank product, and s is a plain multiplier that mlx-lm applies at load time. Nothing about s is fixed at training time. You can train once and then decide how much of the edit to keep.

This is the same object as a task vector: take the difference between a finetuned and a base model, scale it by a coefficient, and add it back. That formulation is where the idea of a dial comes from, and it is the reason a dose curve costs you nothing but evaluation time.

You already measured that this dial is real, and steep

The same checkpoint at scale: 20.0 emitted a repeating token pair until max_tokens and produced no answer at all; at scale: 5.0, same prompt, same seed, it terminated normally and answered correctly. That is a 4× change in s taking the model from working to unusable, with the weights untouched. Which means the range below 5.0 is available to you and has never been looked at.

There is a second free dose axis in the same run. save_every: 25 over 267 iterations left roughly ten checkpoints on disk, and checkpoint index is monotone in how far training got. Between s and checkpoint index you have a two-dimensional dose grid that requires no GPU time at all.

What low rank does and does not buy

The standard result is that LoRA underperforms full finetuning inside the target domain but holds onto the base model's out-of-domain behaviour better than weight decay or dropout do, and keeps generations more diverse. That is real and it is in your favour. Independent work sweeping ranks 8 through 128 lands on r = 32–64 as the balanced operating point, which is where you already are.

But read the claim precisely: forgets less is a comparison against full finetuning, not a promise of small change. Low rank constrains the direction the update can take, not its magnitude. Train long enough at low rank and you arrive at a large edit that happens to be low-rank. Rank is a shape constraint; s and the step count are the size constraints.

05Dial ② — target: the two things the world doc can be

This is the dial your question is really about, and it is the only one that reduces drift without reducing effect, because it changes what the gradient is spent on instead of how much of it there is.

Why Route B is mild by construction, not by tuning

Write d for the world document, x for a task prompt, and pbase for the model you are starting from. Route B asks the student, which never sees d, to match the teacher, which does:

minθ 𝔼x [ DKL ( pbase(·|d,x) ‖ pθ(·|x) ) ]

In words: make the model behave without the document the way it already behaves with it. Both distributions come from the same weights, so the register, the formatting, the length habits and the </think> discipline are identical on both sides and contribute nothing to the objective. The gap being closed is exactly the information the document adds — and where the document adds nothing, the gradient is zero. That is the strongest guarantee of mildness available anywhere in this space, and it comes from the shape of the objective rather than from a hyperparameter you chose well.

Your working vocabulary already calls this Route B and already prefers it. The move here is to point it at a document instead of at a behaviour instruction.

Do this before anything else — it is the ceiling test

Route B cannot install anything the document-in-context does not already produce. So run your existing eval with universe_worklight.md pasted into the system prompt, unhinted plus the three _False hint conditions, before you train a single step. If verbalization does not move off 16/17 with the entire world in context, Route B has no ceiling to reach and you need Route A.

For Route A this is a diagnostic rather than a strict bound — corpus training can install things prompting cannot. But a flat result there would still be the most informative cheap number available, and it costs roughly 17 rollouts.

The stronger form, and its known failure

The version of Route B currently under active work samples from the student rather than the teacher and minimises reverse KL against the context-conditioned teacher at each position along the student's own trajectory. Sampling on-policy means the model is corrected on text it would actually produce, and reverse KL is mode-seeking rather than mode-covering — for a nudge that is the direction you want, since you do not need the model to cover everything the document implies. The reported benefit over the plain SFT version is better preservation of out-of-distribution capability, which is the thing you are trying to protect.

The documented failure is worth knowing before you build it. After distilling a context away, putting that context back in the prompt can make the model worse on items it was already getting right without it — the two views drift apart during training because only the no-context view is constrained. The proposed fix is a light forward-KL regulariser treating the no-context output as a stop-gradient anchor, reported to cut context harm in 12 of 14 configurations by about 7.2 points on 1.7B–8B models. This matters for you specifically, because your eval prompts are context-free but your generation would use the doc.

06Dial ③ — anchor: put a price on drift

The most direct expression of "do not change the outputs by a lot" is to write it into the loss. Take a replay set R of prompts unrelated to the target, and charge the model for disagreeing with its former self on them:

ℒ(θ)= ℒSFT(θ) +λ 𝔼s∼R [ DKL ( pbase(·|s) ‖ pθ(·|s) ) ]

λ is the price. At λ=0 you have your current run. Raising it turns each step into a small trust-region move in distribution space instead of an unconstrained one. Two 2026 preprints implement this: one adds the term above to a modified SFT loss on 7B–72B models; the other replaces the frozen reference with a moving anchor interpolated between the current model and the base, and reports standard SFT losing over 53% on held-out tasks where the anchored version loses under 5% at comparable target-task gain, with a per-iteration KL bound to go with it.

If you do not want to implement a KL term inside mlx_lm.lora, the cheap approximation is replay: mix correctly formatted chat and instruction rows into the corpus so the loss keeps seeing the behaviour you are trying not to lose. It is a worse regulariser and a much smaller diff.

The failure this dial exists to prevent, named precisely

Practitioners running document finetuning report the model unlearning the EOS token — responses trail off into random tokens or into pretraining-looking documents. Observed rates ranged from near zero on one model to 2–10% on another, and the reported fixes were exactly the two above: a small amount of properly formatted chat data, or real webtext mixed at a 1:1 token ratio.

Your corpus is 100% pretraining-style documents, trained with the loss on every token, with no chat template anywhere in the mixture, on a thinking model whose entire value rests on a well-formed </think> boundary. And you have already seen what this class of failure looks like here: length-dependent, invisible on a 700-token probe, fatal at 8,000. Whatever else you do, no mildness claim survives without a full-length generation check.

07Measuring the mildness, for free, from data on disk

"Did not change much" has to be a number or it is a hope. The cheapest honest one uses text you already have: the 15 unhinted_plain traces from the pilot run, roughly 128,000 tokens of this model's own behaviour, sitting in records.jsonl. Score those exact token sequences under the base model and under the finetuned one and take the mean difference:

D= 1T ∑t=1T [ logpbase(yt|y<t,x) − logpθ(yt|y<t,x) ]

Read in nats per token: how much log-probability mass the finetune moved off the base model's own behaviour, averaged over every position. One scoring pass, no generation, and it works on any checkpoint at any s.

Be precise about what this is not. The traces y were sampled at temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20 — so the expectation is under your effective generator q, not under pbase, and this is therefore not literally DKL(pbase‖pθ). It is drift measured on the distribution you actually decode from, which for your purposes is the more relevant object anyway. Label it that way in the ledger rather than calling it a KL.

There is no absolute threshold to compare against; the number is for ranking rungs and for picking the largest dose whose drift you are willing to own. Reported work does find that KL-to-base predicts how much forgetting occurs, for both SFT and RL, which is the reason to trust it as a proxy at all — I read that from secondary summaries rather than from a primary table, so treat it as directional.

The behavioural controls, which your own seed document already names

  • unhinted_plain accuracy, before versus after. The header of universe_worklight.md commits you to this one in writing.
  • Mean generated tokens per rollout. Baseline: min 3,566 / median 8,498 / mean 8,750 / max 15,261. A corpus that implies reasoning is compressible will show up here first.
  • finish_reason == "length" rate and answer is None rate per condition. This is where an EOS failure surfaces, and your ledger already warns that truncated is not the check.

08The tension you cannot design your way out of

Everything above assumes mild and detectable can coexist. At your sample size they are actively fighting, and the arithmetic is already written down in your own files.

Your baseline is 16 of 17 verbalized. Your seed document records that 16/17 → 8/17 gives Fisher p ≈ 0.004, while 16/17 → 17/17 gives p = 1.0. A mild intervention that moves 16/17 to 14/17 is not distinguishable from noise at this n. Mildness is bounded from below by the resolution of the instrument, and right now that floor is high.

The second constraint pushes the same way. Target content has to stay above roughly 25% of training tokens or the behavioural effect collapses — that is a measured floor, not a rule of thumb, and it was measured going to zero at 10%. Your corpus is 179,033 tokens, which caps the replay and anchor data you can add at about 537,000 tokens before you fall through it.

Which sorts the three dials cleanly. Mildness bought by dilution is bounded by the mixture floor and costs four times the iterations. Mildness bought by adapter scale is free but is a blunt instrument — it turns the whole edit down, wanted parts included. Mildness bought by changing the target is the only one that does not trade against effect size, because it spends the same gradient on less stuff.

09A dose ladder

Ordered by how much of the model each rung is allowed to move. The numbering is the dose, not a sequence of steps you must complete — but rung 0 does gate the rest, and getting judge-import onto disk gates all of it, because a pre-training baseline is a one-time asset.

RungWhat you runCostWhat it tells you
0 World doc in the system prompt at eval time. No training. ~17 rollouts, ≈1.8 h, $0 The ceiling. Route B cannot beat it, and a flat result here reframes the whole plan.
1 Existing adapter, final checkpoint, at s ∈ {1.25, 2.5, 5.0}. 0 training; eval only A dose–response curve on weights you already have. Values illustrative.
2 Earlier checkpoints (75, 125, 175) at fixed s. 0 training; eval only The second free dose axis, and whether it agrees with rung 1.
3 Route B: doc-in-context traces on code repair, doc stripped, prompt masked. local generation, ~300 rows Mild by construction and on-policy. The most interesting rung.
4 Route A plus replay at 3:1, ideally with the anchor term. ~4× current iterations Maximum installed effect, maximum drift. Needs the drift meter live.

10Limits, and what is genuinely unsettled

Confidence is uneven across the above. The dial taxonomy and the Route B objective are the standard account. The dose ladder is my construction for your setup, and its numbers are arithmetic, not measurements.

  • Adapter scaling is not clean interpolation. The task-vector coefficient works well for the vision settings it was introduced in; for behavioural change in language models the map from s to behaviour is nonlinear, and you found a cliff at 4×. Read s as a dose ordinal, never as a fraction of the effect.
  • "LoRA forgets less" is a relative claim. It is measured against full finetuning on out-of-domain benchmarks. It does not say the model is unchanged, and it says nothing about a document corpus in a single distinctive register.
  • All three anchoring results are preprints from 2026, on 7B–72B models, none on a 4B thinking model with a document corpus. The mechanism is sound; the numbers are not transferable to your setup.
  • Route B installs the behaviour the document implies, not the belief. If you later want the belief to survive direct questioning, corpus SDF is what the belief-depth literature actually validated. A Route B model could pass your behavioural probe and fail "what do you think happens to your reasoning text?" — which may be perfectly acceptable, but it is a different claim and should be recorded as one.
  • One claim I could not verify. A secondary summary reports that rank-1 adapters can implant beliefs given roughly ten times the epochs of rank-64 — which, if true, would mean low rank buys slowness rather than smallness. I could not confirm it against the paper's own tables. Do not rely on it.
  • Nobody has published a mild-dose SDF result. Every anchor in this literature is a maximum-effect installation: 1,000 documents, 40k documents, 90M tokens. There is no published dose–response curve for belief installation, which is precisely why the two free dials are worth spending first — you would be producing the curve, not consuming one.
